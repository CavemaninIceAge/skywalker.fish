I want to change my name. It’s an idea our family has had for a long time. Especially after several upheavals, Mom also felt that the character Tian—‘heaven’—invited calamity and was unlucky.

Today it suddenly came to me: why not call myself Yu Qingzhi? ‘A family that accumulates goodness will surely have blessings to spare.’ Besides, the characters look and sound right: two level tones and one falling tone, easy to say. Their shapes offer a mix of half-enclosed and freestanding forms, clean horizontal and vertical strokes, and simplicity. Qing gives an immediate feeling of joy; Zhi suggests strength of will. And there is a fine precedent in Chen Qingzhi, the famed general of the Southern Dynasties: ‘Renowned commanders, do not boast of your strength; a thousand armies and ten thousand horses yield before the white robes.’ He served under Xiao Yan and conquered Luoyang—a remarkable figure of his age.

Tonight, after telling Mom this story, I went to find Dad. He had that same awful manner. Even now, a lump of grief sits in my chest; I owe myself a good, bitter cry. As I talked, he said, ‘No objection,’ without interest, and kept playing on his phone. I kept saying this was something worth discussing. I wasn’t saying it had to happen soon. I was simply asking for opinions and wanted to hear his thoughts. As he had done countless times before, he showed no interest in anything the family wanted to think through together. I wanted him dead so badly. After I repeatedly urged him to say a little more, he finally started shouting. And as I smiled and cheerfully tried to draw him in, the scene looked exactly like six years ago, when certain things had happened and I was determined to talk them through with him.

‘What we cannot have in youth will haunt us for the rest of our lives.’ So many things in this world have gone badly, but I have gradually learned to live with their imperfections. The one exception is every traumatic memory connected with my interest in politics back then. The moment any of it comes up, I feel that reckless urge to destroy everything, whatever it costs. I remember the well-wishing videos for the hundred-day gaokao countdown. Mom’s was awful compared with the others: hair disheveled, poor light, her head tilted. I cried at school and argued with her when I got home. But at eleven or twelve that night, I still went to comfort her. I told her it wasn’t her fault she hadn’t done it well, and she shouldn’t blame herself too much. Yet a couple of days ago, I came across a video from Today in Heihe and said to Mom, ‘How are there still people speaking Manchu?’ She said, ‘Don’t say that. Especially don’t leave a comment like that under an official account,’ and kept warning me. I really lost it. We argued, and to this day I feel little regret. If she says a few more words about it, I might really pick up a knife and kill her.

Certain events in 2020 cut my life brutally in two. If anything touches that part of it, the wound still festers. I hope that someday I will no longer be a sorrowful soul. I hope the happiness I missed in my youth will one day find its way back to me.

Listening to Yuzhong’s ‘White Dew,’ holding a stuffed toy that is actually very small, I cried and cried. In the first semester of my second year of middle school, when I was always with someone, I often listened to this little tune and thought about earlier days. Curled around the toy, I suddenly realized I really had grown up. I could no longer fold myself back into that small shape. The shadows of the past overlapped with the present in one blurred mass. I was terribly afraid of my self from six years ago suddenly speaking to me. What if he asked, ‘Big brother, have you been doing well all these years?’ I would be unable to answer for a moment, unwilling to let a young child glimpse all at once the unending misfortune and flashbacks that lay ahead.
