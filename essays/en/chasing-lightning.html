I think anyone with a dream of science is fascinated by such propositions. We do not know whether they are true, but there they are, rich with possible explanations. While reading elementary number theory, I was startled to discover how rigorously primes follow the density 1/ln x. There are so many coincidences in things we take for granted, and we do not even know why. Last night, reading interview advice from a study-abroad agency, I came across the question “Why is the night sky dark?” We extinguish the beginnings of the question with phrases like “the Sun isn’t there,” “interstellar dust,” or “stars losing energy.” A deeper explanation points to a universe that is neither static nor eternal: it has a finite age and is expanding. Light from distant objects either has not reached us yet or has been stretched and diluted by cosmic expansion. This enormous secret hung above everyone’s heads until somebody asked why.

Deep order often hides in everything we take for granted. Discovering and explaining that order produces the science we later call common sense. Inspiration never comes from nowhere. It needs association and a great store of accumulated knowledge; then, at some instant when neurons connect, a spark appears and we catch it. For the great questions, this is like a person catching a bolt of lightning. It is unlikely. But if we never chase the clouds, the probability of lightning grows smaller and smaller until it vanishes.

How should we define this broad word, “creation”? I think it means building foundations and then transferring what we know, discovering that it can solve a richer range of problems. Like Dijkstra’s algorithm, we do not arrive at the destination out of nowhere. We start from what is already established and use existing knowledge to keep updating the paths toward what lies farther away. We cannot find a solution in the next place without a firm, clear grasp of the prerequisites. When I think about the difference between geniuses and the rest of us, beyond greater processing power, perhaps it still comes down to what they have already accumulated. Everyone needs an immense amount of accumulation, like a tireless person chasing a bolt of lightning.

I believe genius exists, and that some people really do have greater processing power and more exceptional minds. But I also believe in accumulation and transfer. In terms of the probability that inspiration strikes us like lightning, the gap between us and clever people who are not once-in-centuries geniuses is far smaller than it seems. Some people who later achieve great things do not lead every early system of assessment. Grades, GPAs, and competition records matter, of course, but they cannot exhaust a person’s possibilities. On the truly long road of scholarship, a feel for problems, resilience, accumulated knowledge, and the ability to transfer it will all reshape a person’s trajectory. Even an IMO gold medal often follows more than a decade of intensive training beginning in primary school. The gulf before me today does not come only from talent. It also comes from the accumulation they have already completed and I have not. I think that beyond talent, what we lack more is accumulated knowledge and time.

One decade has already ended. Although I did not build the mathematical and scientific foundations of those secondary-school competition students, I have made it to Peking University. From today I want to learn those foundations well, like the people who began working through mathematics textbooks every day in primary school. I want to spend the next decade chasing a bolt of lightning.
