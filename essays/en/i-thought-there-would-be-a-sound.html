—Twelve years in the life of a Soviet immigrant child

December 25, 1991. Maryland.

Sasha Goldin, eighteen, was in his basement waiting for a program to finish running. The television was on upstairs. CNN. His father called from the top of the stairs: “Come up. He’s reading it.”

By the time he got upstairs, Gorbachev was halfway through. His voice was flat, as though he were reading a quarterly report. His father sat in the middle of the sofa, holding the remote like a button he had no intention of pressing. Uncle Boris sat at the other end. His mother was in the kitchen. She did not come out.

Outside, it was broad daylight on Christmas Day. An inflatable Santa stood on the neighbors’ lawn across the street, nodding endlessly in the wind. The Goldins did not celebrate Christmas. Theirs was the only television on the street showing Moscow.

At 7:32 p.m. Moscow time, the red flag came down over the Kremlin. CNN cut to a Toyota commercial.

His father said, “I thought there would be a sound.”

———

September 1978. Moscow.

Sasha was five. He remembered three things about that autumn.

The first: his father came home at two in the afternoon. It was a Tuesday. He sat down at the kitchen table in his overcoat and stayed there for a long time without taking it off. It was dark by the time his mother returned from work. His father was still wearing the coat. Later, Sasha learned that this was the day after they had submitted their application to emigrate. The State Planning Committee had taken one morning to fire his father. Before firing him, they held a meeting. Everyone in the department spoke. His father spoke too. What his father said was, “All right.”

The second: Aunt Valya, who used to give him candy, began walking along the other side of the courtyard. There was an iron rocket in the middle, half its red paint flaking off, which he and all the other children climbed. Aunt Valya used to walk on this side of the rocket. Now she walked on the other side. He asked his mother why. His mother said Aunt Valya’s legs hurt.

The third: every evening his father sat in the kitchen with a notebook in front of him and closed his eyes. After a while he opened them, turned a page, and closed them again. His grandmother had said that people closed their eyes when they prayed, so he thought his father was praying. No one in his family prayed. Especially not his father.

It was many years before he learned what this was. Customs regulations forbade taking manuscripts out of the country. His father had three years of theorems in that notebook. The three years of theorems could not leave. A person could. So his father was memorizing them. A thirty-four-year-old mathematician, sitting in his kitchen every evening, committing three years of his own life to memory.

Before he was fired, his father’s job at the State Planning Committee was to prove that Soviet living standards were higher than American ones. A real job. Real mathematics. A proof every quarter. His father later said he did not hate that. Anyone could cook the books. He had done it for eight years and never felt he had lost anything. What he hated was something else. In 1961, at seventeen, he had applied to the physics department at Moscow State University. He wanted to study astrophysics. The physics department had a number that was never put on paper, and that year its quota for Jews was already full. The mathematics department accepted him. His father never once got to look at the stars he wanted to see.

“I could cook their books,” his father said. “They wouldn’t let me look at the stars.”

———

May 1979. Sheremetyevo Airport.

Before leaving, each of them paid five hundred rubles to renounce Soviet citizenship. You had to pay to stop being something. His father said it was the best economics lesson he had ever had.

The three of them queued at customs for four hours. Every family ahead of them had its suitcases searched. When their turn came, a customs officer pulled out his father’s notebook, leafed through it, and asked, “Manuscript?”

“Mathematics.”

“Do you have an export certificate from the Academy of Sciences?”

“No.”

“Then no.” The officer put the notebook into a basket behind the desk. There were already many notebooks in it.

His father said, “All right.”

His mother’s hand rested on his shoulder and squeezed once. He did not know whether the squeeze was meant for him, or whether his mother herself needed something to hold on to.

They went to Vienna. A hotel on the outskirts, Russian filling the corridors, every room containing a family and two suitcases. His father was sitting at the table when Sasha fell asleep the first night. He was still sitting there when Sasha woke the next morning. There was a stack of paper on the table. Hotel stationery. Forty pages, written on both sides. His mother stood in the doorway and said, “Let him write.”

The paper stayed at Sheremetyevo. The theorems reached Vienna.

Then came Italy, a small seaside town called Ladispoli, where all the Soviet Jews waiting for American visas waited. His mother sold a set of bedsheets, a Zenit camera, and two tins of caviar at the market. The Italian buyers bargained hard. They knew the people selling were all desperate to get to America. His father did not go to the market. His father stayed at the hotel and finished page forty-one.

In October 1979, they arrived in Maryland. His father found a position in the mathematics department at the University of Maryland. Two years later, his mother joined NASA Goddard. Sasha started elementary school. His full name was Alexander. The American teachers thought it too long and called him Alex. From then on he was Sasha at home and Alex at school. Two names. One country.

———

March 1983. Maryland.

In Florida, Reagan told a gathering of evangelical ministers that the Soviet Union was an evil empire. The next day, Sasha’s fourth-grade teacher asked, “Alex, you come from the Soviet Union. Why don’t you tell everyone what it’s like inside the evil empire?”

Sasha was nine. He stood up and thought for a moment. He said it snowed there. His grandmother made dumplings, two hundred at a time, and froze them outside the window. There was an iron rocket in the courtyard that you could climb all the way to the top.

The class was disappointed. One boy asked whether he had seen the Gulag. Another asked whether they had McDonald’s. The teacher said, “Thank you, Alex. You can sit down.”

As he sat down, he understood something: Americans did not want the version he remembered. Later, he learned the version they wanted too. Secret police. Queues. No bananas. Every sentence in that version was true. It just wasn’t his. His version contained only snow, dumplings, and an iron rocket. He put his version away. From then on he told only the one they wanted.

That evening, he brought the teacher’s question home. His father was slicing bread. He stopped.

“Evil,” his father said, “is a word from the movies. It isn’t evil. It’s a personnel department. A personnel department that won’t let you look at the stars.”

“Reagan says it’ll fall.”

“Empires, I don’t know. Personnel departments don’t fall. They only change their names.”

His father’s colleagues liked to introduce him at parties by saying, “Lev escaped from the Soviet Union.” His father corrected them every time. “I didn’t escape. I applied. I filled in the forms. They approved them.” No American thought the distinction mattered. His father thought it was the only distinction that mattered. A man who escaped was a hero. A man who applied was a man who had filled in a form. His father did not want to be a hero. The thing he had wanted to be had already been denied him in 1961.

———

July 1990. Moscow.

His father took twelve gifted American mathematics students to the Soviet Union on a two-week exchange. Sasha was sixteen. He went along.

Sheremetyevo again. In 1979 they had searched their suitcases for four hours and taken forty pages of paper. In 1990, the customs officer glanced at the blue booklet and waved them through. As Sasha passed, he looked back at the counter. There was still a basket behind it. He did not know what they put in it now.

Moscow was queuing. The McDonald’s on Pushkin Square had been open for six months. Its line curved from the entrance out onto the street and back again, longer than any line he remembered. His mother used to queue for butter. Now they were queuing for America. His father stood at the end of the line for a long time, watching. Then he led Sasha back the way they had come and stopped in front of a gray building beside the Kremlin.

“I spent eight years in here proving we lived better than the Americans.”

It was a fifteen-minute walk from that building to the end of the McDonald’s line.

They visited their old courtyard on Lenin Avenue. The iron rocket was still there. All the red paint had gone. What remained was deep brown rust. It was much smaller than he remembered. He sat on its second rung with his knees against the third. An old woman on a bench watched them. He did not know whether she was Aunt Valya. He did not ask. Neither did his father.

On the afternoon of the fifth day, he threw a pebble at a police car parked by the road. He did not know why. Perhaps because of 1978. Perhaps simply because he was sixteen. The pebble struck the car door with a small sound. Two policemen got out.

His father walked over. He spoke in rapid Moscow Russian, of which Sasha understood half. The half he understood did not matter. What mattered was that his father’s voice had changed. It had not become quieter. It had become older. It was the voice of that Tuesday in 1978, when he sat in the kitchen wearing his overcoat. His father took a packet of Marlboros from his pocket. The policemen took the cigarettes and returned to the car.

That evening, a man named Gennady Petrovich came to the hotel to see his father. He had heard that Lyova was back. Only people from his father’s old life in Moscow called him Lyova. Gennady had grown fat. He brought a bottle of Armenian brandy and a business card: Progress Cooperative, General Director. He dealt in computers, importing 386s from Singapore. He wanted Lyova to help him find a company in Maryland. He was happy. He kept laughing. He clapped Sasha on the shoulder and said, “See? We’re all businessmen now.” His father had two drinks with him. They shook hands.

After Gennady left, Sasha asked, “Was he the one who—”

“Yes.”

“The one who chaired that meeting in 1978.”

“Yes.”

“You shook his hand.”

“He doesn’t remember. You can’t settle accounts with someone who doesn’t remember.”

“Does he really not remember?”

His father thought for a moment. “He remembers the meeting. What he doesn’t remember is there being anything wrong with it. Those are two different things.”

On the flight home, as they crossed the Baltic Sea, Sasha said the sentence he had been preparing for five days: “Thank you for getting us out.”

His father looked out the window. A long time passed.

“Don’t thank me. I didn’t leave for you. I left for myself. You came along.”

Another silence.

“What frightened me most was never that they wouldn’t let us go. It was that they would, and I’d discover it was the same outside.”

“Is it?”

“The personnel departments outside don’t care whether you look at the stars. That’s the only difference.” His father pulled down the window shade. “That difference is enough.”

———

August 19, 1991. Maryland.

The news of the coup arrived in the morning. His father sat in front of the television for three days. He did not shave for three days, which was a serious matter in the Goldin household. They tried calling Moscow. They could not get through. Every line was busy. Sasha had a summer job at a computer shop near his school. Every day when he came home, his father was still in the same place.

On the second evening his father said, “If they win.”

“We weren’t going back anyway.”

“Whether you go back is one thing. Whether you can go back is another.”

Sasha did not understand the difference then. He was seventeen, with a blue passport. There was nowhere in the world he could not go.

On August 21, the coup failed. The tanks withdrew. His father switched off the television and said, “It’s not over yet.”

———

December 25, 1991.

Now. Now Uncle Boris sat at the other end of the sofa.

Uncle Boris and his father had submitted their applications on the same day in 1978. His father’s was approved. Boris’s was not. The reason given was “access to classified information.” He had worked for three years in a “mailbox,” one of those research institutes with no name, only a post-office box number. After he was fired, he became a boiler operator, heating a residential district for thirteen years. In the spring of 1991, the door opened. Anyone could leave. He arrived in Maryland in September and stayed in the Goldins’ guest room, seeming perpetually jet-lagged, looking at everything twice.

When the red flag came down, Boris watched it twice. Then he said, “If I’d known, I’d have waited another four months.”

No one laughed. Then his father did, briefly.

The telephone rang. It was Bob, the department chair. Bob said, “Lev, you must be so happy. Merry Christmas!” His father said, “Thank you, Bob.” After hanging up, he said in Russian, “He thinks I’ve won a ball game.”

His mother came out of the kitchen with a pot of soup, set it on the table, glanced at the tricolor on the television, and went back to the kitchen. That evening she said just one thing: “I finished watching this at customs in 1979.”

Sasha stood behind the sofa. He felt nothing. He had tried. He had tried to feel something, the way he had tried to talk about the Gulag in fourth grade. He knew what this day meant to the three adults in the room. He knew every detail: the physics department in 1961, the meeting in 1978, the forty pages, the thirteen years of boilers. He knew. But he stood there as though he were watching news about someone else’s family. He was American. He was eighteen. He had a program running in the basement, calculating a table of prime numbers. No one had told him to calculate it.

Without turning around, his father said, “You don’t feel anything, do you?”

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be sorry.” His father still did not turn around. “This is the one thing I’ve achieved in my life.”

At two in the morning, Sasha came downstairs for a glass of water. The study light was on. His father sat at the desk with a yellow legal pad and a pencil, working something out. On that Tuesday evening in September 1978, his father had done mathematics in the kitchen. On their first night in Vienna in May 1979, his father had done mathematics. On December 25, 1991, the Soviet Union was gone. His father was doing mathematics.

Sasha stood in the doorway and watched for a while. He remembered someone his father had mentioned: Andrei Sakharov, who had died two years earlier and had not lived to see this day. Sakharov had been allowed everything—physics, the hydrogen bomb, medals—and then he had handed it all back. His father had been allowed nothing, so he had nothing to hand back. His father had said he did not know which of the two was luckier.

Sasha switched off the hall light and went upstairs. It would be many years before he could put into words what he learned that night: his father had never escaped from the Soviet Union. His father had simply taken the one thing they could not confiscate and moved house.

———

February 24, 2022. Maryland.

The same sofa. Reupholstered twice. Never moved.

Lev Goldin was seventy-eight. He still held the remote in his hand. CNN. There were sounds in the sky above Kyiv.

Sasha was forty-eight, sitting beside him. Half his hair had gone gray too. He owned a company on the West Coast. His mother had been dead five years. Uncle Boris had died the year before. At five that morning his father had called and said only, “Sasha. Come home.” He had flown back from San Francisco just to sit in this spot.

This time, he felt everything.

His father said, “This time there’s a sound.”

From that day on, they could not go back. It was no longer a matter of choosing not to. They could not. It had taken him thirty-one years to understand the difference his father had described in August 1991.

The gray building where his father had cooked the books for eight years had been called the State Duma since 1994. The people voting in that building this week sat in the same rooms where his father had once proved their standard of living. The personnel department had changed its name for the third time.

His father watched for five minutes and turned off the television. Sasha had never seen him turn it off. In 1991 he had not turned it off. He had waited twelve years. He could not turn it off. Today he did. He had waited twelve years and heard no sound. The sound had arrived thirty-one years late. He listened for five minutes.

His father stood up and went into the study. He left the door ajar.

Sasha sat alone on the sofa. This time, he was the one in the middle. The one thing his father had achieved in his life had lasted thirty-one years. Today it expired.

From the study came the sound of a pencil. The kitchen in 1978. Vienna in 1979. Christmas in 1991. The year 2022. All his life, his father had trusted only one sound.
