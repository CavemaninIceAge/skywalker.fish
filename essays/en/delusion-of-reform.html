—Thirteen Years in the Life of a Moscow Engineer

December 25, 1991. Moscow.

Anatoly Grebnev, fifty-one, sat in front of the television. A bag rested on the stool by the door. Inside were a toothbrush, two pairs of underpants, a sweater, and a book he had not finished. The bag had been there since 1983. The toothbrush had been replaced three times.

Gorbachev was reading. Grebnev wasn't listening to the words; he was watching Gorbachev's hands. The hands holding the speech were steady. His own hands had not been steady for twelve years.

His illness was written in his medical record. Delusions of reform.

———

March 1979. Moscow.

Grebnev was thirty-nine, an inspector in the Technical Inspection Department of the Red Proletarian Machine Tool Plant. Stamp No. 41. Before a machine left the factory, he was its last stop: he looked, he measured, he stamped. The stamp was round, with two lines in the center: “Technical Inspection,” “41.” A stamped machine was a machine that met the standard. This was his job, and also how he understood the world: to establish what a thing was, someone had to look at it, measure it, and stamp it.

For the fourth quarter of 1978, the factory reported 112 percent fulfillment of its production plan. Grebnev had counted: a third of the machines dispatched that quarter had parts missing—feed gearboxes, protective guards, motors. The machines were shipped to other factories, which had to supply the parts themselves. Those they couldn't complete were left in the yard. A third of that 112 percent consisted of iron shells.

He would not stamp machines with missing parts. The workshop manager took his stamp and stamped them for him.

He began writing letters. The first to the factory Party committee, the second to the ministry, the third to Pravda, the fourth to the Central Committee, the fifth to the People's Control Committee, the sixth to the procurator's office. They were all short, one page, nothing but numbers: machines dispatched, machines missing parts, which parts, serial numbers of the incomplete machines. He used no adjectives. He thought the numbers were enough.

One of the six letters received a reply. Pravda sent him a printed sheet: Your letter has been forwarded to the relevant authorities for action. The relevant authorities were the factory Party committee. They stapled the letter into his file, alongside his 1975 certificate of merit as an outstanding worker.

He never finished the seventh letter. On March 19, two men came from the procurator's office. The charge was Article 190-1 of the Criminal Code: dissemination of knowingly false fabrications defaming the Soviet state system. The fabrications were those numbers. First, they said, he would undergo an examination. The examination would be at the Serbsky Institute. When he left, he put his stamp in his pocket. He didn't know why. He just took it.

———

April 1979. The Serbsky Institute.

The doctor who questioned him was a woman of about fifty, very pleasant. She asked why he had written the letters. He said because the numbers were false. She asked how many he had written. Six, he said. She asked whether he believed he was the only one who had noticed. No, he said, everyone in the factory could see it. Then why, she asked, were you the only one who wrote?

He thought for a moment and said he didn't know.

She asked him to explain a proverb: All that glitters is not gold. He said it meant that not everything with a stamp met the standard. She wrote that down. She asked him to explain another: One swallow does not make a spring. Neither does one letter, he said. She wrote that down too.

She wrote on the paper. He would not see what she had written until thirteen years later. Diagnosis: sluggish schizophrenia. Symptoms: delusions of reform; an obstinate pursuit of truth; an inflated assessment of his own personality; a lack of critical insight into his own condition.

An obstinate pursuit of truth. That was a symptom.

In May, the assessment committee reached its conclusion: not criminally responsible; compulsory treatment recommended. He was not present at the court hearing. People deemed not responsible did not have to attend.

———

1980. Kazan.

The special psychiatric hospital was run by the Ministry of Internal Affairs. Walls, barbed wire, watchtowers—just like a labor camp, except that the people inside had no fixed sentences. A sentence had a number. Treatment did not.

The orderlies were prisoners. A doctor came once a week. The medicine was haloperidol, with nothing to counteract its side effects. Once you took it, you could neither sit still nor stand steadily; you could only walk back and forth until your legs cramped, then sit, find you couldn't sit, and walk again. Those who disobeyed were injected with sulfazin—sulfur suspended in oil, shot into the buttock, raising a fever of forty degrees for three days. Grebnev received it twice. The first time because he told the doctor he wasn't ill. The second because he told the doctor he was.

He learned to say nothing.

The man who taught him shared his ward. His name was Kuzmin; he had been there seven years. Kuzmin really was ill. He believed he was Lenin's grandson and wrote to the Central Committee every morning, then tore the letter up. Kuzmin told him: don't speak to the doctors. They aren't here to listen. They're here to record. Everything you say becomes a symptom. If you say nothing, all they can record is “silent.” Silence isn't a symptom. Silence counts as improvement.

Grebnev thought: the only person here who has given me useful advice is a man who believes he is Lenin's grandson.

Lyudmila visited twice. The second time, she cried. She said the doctor had spoken to her, said the illness was hereditary, said Olya was fifteen and would one day need to go to university, to marry. “Tolya,” she said, “I have to think of Olya.” He said, “Then think of her.”

They divorced in 1981. He signed the papers with a shaking hand. It took three attempts to get his signature inside the box.

———

1983. Moscow.

He was discharged. Nobody told him why, just as nobody had told him why he had been put there. The discharge certificate read: condition in remission; transfer to outpatient observation. Outpatient observation meant being registered at the district psychiatric dispensary, reporting once a month, being barred from driving, traveling abroad, carrying a gun, and returning to his former work.

On the fifteenth of every month, he went to the dispensary. The nurse called his name. He went in. The doctor asked three questions: Are you sleeping well? Have you had any thoughts? Are you still writing letters? He answered: well, no, no. The doctor wrote “stable” in the register. Nine years, 108 visits, three questions, three answers.

He found work loading and unloading at a vegetable depot. Potatoes arrived with thirty percent rotten; the books recorded forty percent. Nobody knew where the extra ten percent went. He saw it. He said nothing. He felt this was not cowardice. It was the treatment working.

Every year, two weeks before November 7, two weeks before May Day, and whenever a foreign head of state visited, the dispensary sent a vehicle to collect the registered patients and hospitalize them for half a month. This was called preventive hospitalization. The first year, he hadn't known. When the vehicle came, he had brought nothing and spent half a month wearing someone else's trousers. From the second year on, he kept a bag by the door.

The November ward always held the same handful of people: a Baptist, a man who had written a letter about Afghanistan, a man who wanted to emigrate, a man who really was mad. They nodded to one another, said nothing, stayed half a month, went home. Like a class reunion.

In December 1986, Sakharov returned to Moscow from Gorky. The station was full of reporters; the arrival was televised live. Grebnev watched on the television in the loaders' dormitory. The next day he reported to the dispensary. The nurse was the same nurse, the register the same register. Sakharov was an academician. The whole world knew his name. Grebnev was an inspector who had written six letters. Nobody knew his name, so nobody released him.

He understood something: freedom was handed out by name. People without names stood at the back of the queue.

In the spring of 1988, Olya came to see him at the vegetable depot. She was twenty-four and getting married. “Dad,” she said, “don't come to the wedding. His family doesn't know, and I don't want them to.” As she spoke, she kept looking at the potatoes on the ground. “All right,” he said. From beneath his pillow he took an envelope containing the four hundred rubles he had saved and gave it to her. She took it. As she left, she glanced back at him. He remembered that look for a long time, unable to tell whether it held apology or something else. He felt that didn't matter. She had taken the money. If she took the money, she was still his daughter.

———

November 7, 1991. Moscow.

He woke at six in the morning and went to check the bag by the door. It was there. He straightened it.

Tanks had entered the city that August, then left. At the end of August, the Party had been suspended. Every day people on television talked about the past. They sounded like his letters, only with adjectives. He wasn't sure whether the vehicle would still come this year. He waited.

It hadn't come by eight. It hadn't come by ten. At noon, he boiled potatoes, ate them, and sat back down on the stool by the door. At three in the afternoon he heard a car downstairs and stood up. It was the neighbor's son.

Darkness fell. He opened the bag, took out the toothbrush, and put it back in the cup. The toothbrush had spent eight years in the bag. The cup had been empty all that time.

On November 15, he went to the dispensary as usual. There were few people in the corridor. The doctor asked whether he was sleeping well. He said yes. There were dark circles beneath the doctor's eyes, as though he hadn't slept in three days. Grebnev wanted to ask him the same question. He didn't.

———

December 25, 1991.

Now. Now Gorbachev had finished reading.

The flag over the Kremlin came down. Grebnev was thinking about those words in his medical record. Delusions of reform. The whole country had come down with it this year. There weren't enough beds to treat them all.

He was neither happy nor unhappy. He was considering a technical question: if an entire country met the criteria for a diagnosis, did it still count as a diagnosis?

———

August 1992. Moscow.

A new law had passed in July, the Law on Psychiatric Care. One provision stated: psychiatry must not be used for nonmedical purposes. The dispensary sent out a notice. Those on the register could apply for reassessment.

There were three people on the reassessment committee, two old and one young. The young one was a woman doctor, about thirty. She read his medical record from beginning to end. When she reached the 1979 page, she paused for a long time. He saw her lips move slightly, mouthing “an obstinate pursuit of truth.” She did not smile. She turned the file around, pushed it toward him, and said, “You have the right to see this.” He read it. Delusions of reform. An obstinate pursuit of truth. An inflated assessment of his own personality. A lack of critical insight into his own condition. He read it twice. Not badly written, he thought. Three of the four are true. She looked up at him.

“Mr. Grebnev, do you still believe those numbers were false?”

“They were false.”

“Do you still write letters?”

“Not anymore.”

“Why?”

He thought for a moment. “Nobody needs my stamp anymore.”

She wrote a line on the final page of the file and signed it. The two older doctors signed too. The nurse brought the file over for his signature. The line read: diagnosis withdrawn; mentally healthy.

He signed. His hand shook. It took two attempts. On the second, he got it inside the box.

———

When he got home, he opened a drawer. Inside was a stamp: “Technical Inspection 41.” The one he had put in his pocket on March 19, 1979. They had confiscated it in Kazan, returned it when he was discharged, and it had spent nine years in the drawer. The ink had dried. He breathed on the stamp and pressed it onto the reassessment certificate the dispensary had given him, beside the words “mentally healthy.”

A faint circle. The words in the center were legible.

Since March 1979, nobody had asked him to certify anything. This was the first time. He put the stamp back in the drawer.

There was no bag on the stool by the door. The bag was in the cupboard. The toothbrush was in the cup.

———

(The End)
