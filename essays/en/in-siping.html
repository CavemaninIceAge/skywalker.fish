Siping again. We seldom describe a city as tragically heroic, yet the straight, level strokes of the two characters in “Siping” already suggest a resolve to yield not an inch of ground, just as the name “Xuzhou” evokes the horror of a landscape strewn with bodies.

In the defensive battle of Siping, whole companies of our soldiers died in just ten days. Though a section of the position was lost each day, the enemy’s advance grew no faster. By the time Siping fell, the Fourth Field Army had lost more than half its strength. In return, the Nationalists’ elite forces had been held south of the Liao River and temporarily deprived of the ability to organize a large-scale offensive.

And here I am, staying in a thirty-yuan-a-night guesthouse opposite the station, listening to the drizzle. Wind, rain sounds, and light pour through cracks in the walls of this windowless room. The old women are still calling out to passersby, trying to persuade them to stay. In this cage of yellowed wallpaper on all four sides, the color reminds me of the Fourth Field Army’s uniforms.

Less than a century ago, on rainy nights like this, Communist cadres and Red Army soldiers in thin padded jackets boarded ships in Shandong and landed on this territory said to “determine the fate of China’s civil war.” Despite exclusion by their Soviet comrades, they assembled their first military forces. Many could not have imagined how madly death would sweep the battlefield. Perhaps they had imagined it. But the front before them determined the situation in the Northeast, and the Northeast determined the war across the country. Only through bloodshed could they choose, between two paths, a new life for China.

What they faced was General Du Yuming, American-equipped divisions several times their own strength, an uncertain base of popular support in the Northeast, and the Soviets’ shifting attitudes in the great cities... Yet they went forward without turning back, with the determination and faith to outplay fate by half a move. Even today, through the towering sheen of water, I still cannot clearly see what sustained them on those nights. On the page, the dream of a realm of freedom seems so insubstantial. Did sacrifice give it its true weight?

People laugh at those who do not know their own limits. But are not the stories that shake us created by people who refuse to accept their fate? So we cry out, believing that if more people awaken, there must still be hope of breaking this iron house. So we look up from a deep well at the starry night, believing that one day we will see the whole sky we are supposedly fated to see only as a circle. So we must go out, even if we wander into the void. So we wake from despondency, choosing pain over numbness. We do not know what lies ahead. There is only going forward, undeterred...

Here in Siping, I listen to the rain and wait for dawn, hoping the water can wash away my weakness. After the downpour, I will learn that my faith has not been shattered.
