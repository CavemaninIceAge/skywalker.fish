I can’t believe I’m nineteen. My eighteenth birthday is still vivid. That day I was diagnosed with severe pneumonia and admitted to hospital, right in my final year of high school. Bored at night, I happened to read an essay in a literary magazine with a perhaps overblown title: “Only on Returning Home Did I Realize That Leaving Childhood Behind Was the Unbearable Weight.” In the quiet of the night, it felt like a sudden revelation. Every kind of feeling came over me.

Following ‘Sixteen Hundred’ on his journey home, the essay explores his own and his family’s fate and the meaning of a hometown—not only the starting point of memory, but a symbol of loss and growth. Faced with an accelerating age and a disappearing rural world, we have to ‘live our way out,’ discovering ourselves and the meaning of life anew through understanding where we came from.

There is a passage I can still recite accurately:

“Most arguments on the subway break out among people without seats. We often roll our eyes at their noise, easily forgetting that if they had seats, none of it would have happened… the words he gave me: live your way out. For the first time, I realized that life could be an art of possibility, and that I could become an ‘artist’ beyond the jurisdiction of adjectives… seeing yourself as a survivor, not a victim… From the moment you resolve to ‘live your way out,’ your opponent is time. It compresses your past and present, and determines your ‘afterward.’”

Even now, on Peking University’s campus, I don’t think of myself as someone favored by fate. My high-school entrance exam went worse than any of my mock exams, and so did the gaokao—not to mention the series of struggles from my fourth year of middle school to my second year of high school that those closest to me know so well. Of course there are many ways to measure success. But on the commonly accepted scale, I have rarely won. Often, at those moments when “you’ve only got one shot,” I haven’t even managed my own average. On that first day in hospital, having barely finished my birthday cake before going to the Forestry General Hospital for scans and a diagnosis, I seriously doubted whether I was someone who would have an afterward. I wanted so badly to prove myself, even if not for the future, then at least to write a beautiful final chapter to the aftermath of something that happened in my fourth year of middle school. I think the words I carried away from that birthday were ‘live your way out’ and ‘afterward.’

The reality was that I spent a month in hospital. The usual pressures remained, while I seemed to drift further and further from the starry sky of my childhood. Then, in February of the new year, a notice about the final opportunity to confirm gaokao subject choices caught my attention. I staked everything on switching to the humanities and taking the history-track exam. What followed may be an ordinary story. In this completely new field, I shook off my numbness, raced ahead, and found a roundabout way through. This year, somehow, improbably but stubbornly, I made it into Peking University.

Here on campus today, I have set one foot into the promised ‘afterward,’ perhaps brushed by a faint glimmer of fortune. There may still be another roundabout mission ahead: finding my way back into science and engineering. The road is long, a homeward journey of ten thousand miles. Looking back from my first dim stirrings of affection to the life I lead now, I see mountain ranges upon mountain ranges, great peaks and rivers. But rereading that essay at the threshold of nineteen, this is the passage that catches my eye:

“Nostalgia is an aesthetics of loss, and perhaps a ‘hometown’ is something made to be lost—in reality, and in our field of vision. I cannot put a frame around the wind turbines on the distant hills. But as long as you do not feel contempt or fear, ‘Sixteen Hundred’ can still be found. Do not forget: the ‘Sixteen Hundred of the woods’ and the ‘professor of the academy,’ from before and after you ‘lived your way out,’ together shape your way of seeing the world. And by looking closely at them, you find a way to understand this land.”

How should I understand the twists and turns of my life that the people I love know so well? Perhaps life is like climbing up a mountain and coming back down. How high or low you are at a given moment isn’t so important; no one can stay in one position forever anyway. What matters more may be the journey continuing, and our own progress, humility, and sincerity. Besides, someone determined to ‘live their way out’ can always reach an ‘afterward.’ And as long as you feel ‘neither fear nor contempt,’ that young, unformed self can always be found again. All the thoughts that passed through those years together shape your way of seeing the world. By looking closely at them, you find a way to understand life.

May my first year at Peking University be happy, and may everything turn out even better than I hope!
