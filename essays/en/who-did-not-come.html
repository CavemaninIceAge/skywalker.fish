—Thirty years in the life of a Tajik teacher of Russian

December 25, 1991. Dushanbe.

Gulnora Nazarova, forty-one, was filling in her attendance register at the kitchen table. It was the last day of term. She had to add up everyone’s absences and enter the totals in the final column.

A shipping container stood in the courtyard. A green railway container, three tons, there since last week. Maria Semyonovna’s son had come from Novosibirsk. Mother and son had spent a week loading it. This afternoon they finished. The things that would not fit stood beside it: a sewing machine, a chair, ten volumes of Pushkin.

Snow was falling in Dushanbe. Here it melted as soon as it touched the ground. The spines of the Pushkin volumes were already wet.

Maria Semyonovna was upstairs watching television. When Gulnora went up, Gorbachev had already begun reading, in the language Gulnora had taught for twenty years. Maria Semyonovna sat on a chair that had not gone into the container. There was nothing left in the room but the television and the chair. Tomorrow the television would belong to a neighbor too.

By the time the flag came down over the Kremlin, Dushanbe was completely dark.

Maria Semyonovna said, “Well, that settles it. Now they’re both gone.”

———

1961. A village near Kulob.

Gulnora was eleven. Her grandmother was sixty, could not read, and had a scar on her face.

The scar dated from the late 1920s. That year Soviet officials had come to the village and told the women to burn their veils. Her grandmother burned hers. So did her grandmother’s younger sister. Three months later, her husband’s brother stoned the sister to death beside an irrigation canal. The mark on her grandmother’s face was from that same day. The man throwing stones at her had missed his aim.

So for her grandmother, the Soviets were not something far away in Moscow. They were the thing that had kept her alive.

The village school had a Russian teacher, Maria Semyonovna, twenty-nine, from Leningrad. In 1942, when she was ten, she and her mother had escaped across the ice of Lake Ladoga, then traveled a month by train to Stalinabad. Her mother fell ill on the train and died three weeks after they arrived. Maria grew up in an orphanage, graduated from teacher training, and was assigned to teach Russian in a village near Kulob. She had never returned to Leningrad. She said there was nobody left there.

Maria Semyonovna taught them to recite Pushkin’s “Winter Evening.” “The storm shrouds the sky in darkness, whirling up great eddies of snow.” Gulnora learned it quickly. She had never seen snow like that. Winter in Kulob was gray, not white. When she recited it, she could not picture it, so she imagined a very big dust storm.

That November, Stalinabad was renamed Dushanbe. Maria Semyonovna wrote the new name on the blackboard and said, “You’ll go to teacher training here.”

Gulnora’s father said girls did not go.

Maria Semyonovna brought the collective farm chairman to their house. The chairman sat for an hour and smoked half a pack of cigarettes. In the end, her father said all right.

The Soviet state had argued with her father on her behalf. She had won.

———

September 1980. Nurek.

Gulnora was thirty, teaching Russian at Dushanbe’s Secondary School No. 41. Her husband, Davlat, was an engineer at the Nurek hydroelectric plant and came home once a week. That year the dam was completed: three hundred meters high, the tallest in the world.

Davlat took her to see the turbine hall. Pointing to the humming machinery, he said the electricity generated here went to Uzbekistan, to all of Central Asia, to her classroom. He was proud when he said it. She was proud too. Her son was two, her daughter still in her belly. She had a two-room apartment in Dushanbe, a balcony with a view of the mountains, and a class of thirty-two students.

At home they spoke Russian. Davlat had gone to university in Moscow, and their Tajik was not the same: his came from the Nurek mountains, hers from Kulob, and it was hard for the children to follow. Russian was the road in between, equally wide whoever traveled it.

In October her class went to the Vakhsh Valley to pick cotton. It was the same every year from September to November: the whole school moved to the cotton fields, teachers included. On cotton-picking days the attendance register was blank. Nobody was absent. The entire school was elsewhere.

Later, she thought those might have been the best years of her life. The state gave her electricity, gave her an apartment, gave her a class, and made her students kneel in the fields for two months every year. Back then she saw no contradiction between those things. Back then she did not think she had to choose.

———

July 1989. Dushanbe.

Tajik became the state language. A directive reached the school: Russian lessons were to be reduced from six periods a week to four. During the departmental meeting, Safar, the Tajik teacher, patted her shoulder and said, Don’t worry. There are still four.

She was not worried. She was worried about other things. When school began in September, a girl with a Russian name did not come. The girl’s mother came to collect her records and said they were moving to Samara. She did not look at Gulnora as she said it. Gulnora drew an “A” in the register. Absent.

On the afternoon of February 12, 1990, she walked home from school past the square in front of the Central Committee building. It was full of people, all young, all Tajik. Someone was shouting that Armenians had taken their apartments. She did not know which Armenians or which apartments. In the crowd she saw Rahim from her class: fifteen, third row, poor at Russian composition, good at mathematics. He had a stone in his hand. He threw it at a bus.

The next day, eleven of her thirty students came. She drew nineteen “A”s.

On the third day, Rahim’s father came to the school. He had not come for records. He asked if she had a photograph of Rahim. They had none at home of him alone. She found the class photograph, cut Rahim out, and gave him to his father. In Rahim’s column in the attendance register she drew an “A,” then did not know on which day she should stop.

In March, parents with Russian names came one after another to collect records. Of thirty-two names, seven were gone. All Russian names.

Maria Semyonovna was fifty-eight that year. She had retired three years earlier but still taught as a substitute. After March, only two Russian teachers remained in the department: Maria and Gulnora. Maria Semyonovna said, “It was like this when I arrived in 1942. The railway station was full of boxes. Only the direction was reversed.”

Later she understood something: there was no column in an attendance register labeled “The country is gone.” There was only “A.” History was not on television. History was who did not come.

———

December 25, 1991.

Now. Now Maria Semyonovna sat on a chair, with nothing left in the room.

She was fifty-nine. Her son Alyosha was an engineer in Novosibirsk. Since the August coup he had called three times and said, Mum, you have to leave. She said, Where am I supposed to go? Alyosha said, Come here. She said, I’ve never been to Novosibirsk. Alyosha said, Mum, you’ve never been anywhere else either.

She was evacuated twice in her life. The first time was from Leningrad, aged ten, sitting in a truck crossing Lake Ladoga. The truck ahead fell through a hole in the ice. The second time was from the Soviet Union, aged fifty-nine, sitting on a chair watching television. Neither time did anybody ask her.

In September Leningrad had been renamed Saint Petersburg. She saw it in the newspaper, cut out the item, and slipped it inside volume one of Pushkin. In December the Soviet Union disappeared. That was what she meant by “they’re both gone.” She was a Leningrader and a Soviet citizen. By this evening, both words were in the past tense. She was going to a city she had never visited, to become something she had never been: a Russian.

Gulnora asked her what she would do with Pushkin.

“Too heavy. The container only holds three tons. Books weigh the most.” She glanced at the television. “You take them. You still have those four lessons to teach anyway.”

Gulnora carried the ten volumes downstairs. Their spines were wet. She put them on her own kitchen table beside the attendance register. She had not yet finished the final column.

She sat down and counted. In September 1989 this class had thirty-two names. Today she counted nineteen. Of the thirteen missing, eleven had Russian names and had left. One was a girl who had married. One was Rahim. She had not stopped marking him absent until June.

The television was showing the tricolor. She closed the register.

The next morning the container was taken away. Maria Semyonovna rode to the airport in Alyosha’s car without looking back at the building. Standing on the balcony, Gulnora saw that the chair that would not fit was still in the snow. By noon the chair was gone too. Someone had taken it.

———

May 1992. Dushanbe.

Two squares. The president’s people were in Freedom Square; the opposition was in Martyrs’ Square. Martyrs’ Square was the one where Rahim had thrown the stone, renamed for the people who died in February 1990. A few streets separated the two squares. Each held rallies for two months. In May they began shooting at one another.

The school closed on May 10. There was no announcement. She simply arrived that morning to find the door locked, and the old gatekeeper sitting outside. Go home, Teacher Gulnora, he said. Today is not a day for lessons. She asked when it would be. The old man said, How should I know?

Davlat was in Nurek. He had not come home. The dam was still running. On the phone he said that people from both sides had come to the dam, all carrying guns, all asking whose dam it was. He told them: The dam belongs to electricity. Whoever opens fire will have no power at home. Both sides left. The dam did not care who was on top.

On December 10, men from Kulob entered the city. They called themselves the Popular Front. The next day, the knocking began at the doors of Pamiris and people from Gharm.

A checkpoint appeared at the end of the street. The men there looked at her papers, turned to the page listing her birthplace, Kulob, and said, Sister, you’re one of us. Go ahead. For the first time in her life, being born in Kulob had made her safe. One of us. She had taught Russian for twenty years and had never taught those words from a textbook.

Niso Shakirova taught mathematics at their school. She was a Pamiri from Khorog and had taught for fourteen years. On December 14 she did not come to school. The school was half open, half closed then; whoever turned up was enough. On the fifteenth she did not come. On the sixteenth Gulnora went to her apartment, fourth floor. The door was open. Nobody was inside. Everything was still there, and her attendance register lay on the table. A neighbor said that the night before, a car with no license plates had come.

Gulnora took Niso’s attendance register home and placed it beside her own, beside Pushkin. Thirty-one names, in Niso’s handwriting. She turned to the last page. There was no column for the teacher. She did not know where to put the “A.”

———

September 1993. Dushanbe.

School reopened. Four girls came to her class. In 1989 there had been fifteen.

The other girls were all at home. Some were married, at sixteen or seventeen. Some fathers said that with things as they were, it was unsafe for girls to go out. Some said that with things as they were, what was the use of learning Russian? She went from house to house. Some fathers let her in; some did not. The collective farm chairman was gone. Nobody accompanied her to sit for an hour and smoke half a pack of cigarettes.

Only then did she understand: the thing that had argued with her father for her in 1961 was truly gone.

Her daughter Zarina married in 1999, at eighteen, just after finishing secondary school. Gulnora did not object. She wanted to, but she could not think of a reason. She wanted to say, You can go to teacher training. But if you graduated and became a teacher, a month’s salary would not buy three kilograms of meat. She wanted to say, Don’t rush. But it was not Zarina who was rushing. It was the life Zarina had to live.

Her son Farrukh went to Moscow in 2000, to work on a building site. There were no building sites in Dushanbe.

———

January 2023. Dushanbe.

Gulnora was seventy-three. Davlat had died ten years earlier. The dam was still running.

Her grandson Sukhrob was twenty and going to Moscow, like his uncle. Russia now required exams: to get a work permit, you had to pass Russian, history, and law. He had an app on his phone with a bank of a thousand questions. He came to his grandmother because she was a Russian teacher, because his Russian was poor, because she was the only person with whom he had ever spoken Russian.

He showed her a history question: In what year did the Great Patriotic War end? She said 1945. He said, Grandma, how do you know? She said, The person who taught me Russian came out of Leningrad.

She took Pushkin down from the shelf. Thirty-two years had passed, and the watermarks from that snow were still on the spines. She turned to “Winter Evening.”

“Grandma, this isn’t on the exam.”

“I know.”

“The exam asks things like ‘Please show your papers.’”

“I know. Read this first.”

He read it. Not well. “The storm shrouds the sky in darkness, whirling up great eddies of snow.” He did not know what an eddy was. He had never seen that kind of snow either. In Dushanbe, snow melted as soon as it touched the ground.

She thought of 1961, of herself reciting those lines in a classroom in Kulob, unable to imagine them, imagining a very big dust storm instead. Of Maria Semyonovna writing a new name on the blackboard and saying, You’ll go here. Of the collective farm chairman’s half pack of cigarettes.

The language she had taught all her life had first been a way for people to stay. The last time, it was a way for them to leave.

Sukhrob left in February. In March he sent a photograph from Moscow: the fence around a building site, and beyond the fence nothing could be seen clearly. He said, Grandma, this is it. Eddies.

At seventy-three, she saw the snow from the poem for the first time.

(The End)
