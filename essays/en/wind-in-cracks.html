—Forty Years in the Life of a Naval Researcher

December 2043. Qingdao.

Lu Weiming stood outside the old teaching building of the Naval Submarine Academy, watching workers take down the red-lettered banner above the entrance: “Follow the Party's Command. Be Able to Win Wars.” He had looked at those words for thirty-two years—they had been hanging there on his first day as a student in 1987. A new banner went up when he returned to teach in 1995, and another when he became head of the Propulsion Department in 2014.

Now the workers were scraping away the double-sided tape with small knives, layer by layer, exposing the faded white tiles beneath. There was a mark on the tiles—not from this slogan, but from an earlier time, when a different slogan had hung here. He no longer remembered what it had said. Only the words “national defense” and “modernization” remained; time had erased the rest.

He had come to complete his retirement paperwork. Sixty-two: he could retire now. The young woman in the personnel office could not find his name in the system—not because it was broken, but because the old system had been shut down three months ago, and his file had yet to be transferred to the new one. The Ministry of National Defense was being renamed the Ministry of National Defense and Maritime Affairs. The navy, army, and air force were all being reorganized; the files of every technical officer below deputy corps level were caught in the transition.

“Professor Lu, right now we can only retrieve fragments of your electronic file. Your new officer number hasn't come through yet—perhaps you could come back another day.”

“That's all right. I'm in no hurry.”

He left the building and stood on the paved campus road. Thirty-two years ago, it had been a cinder path. Now it was asphalt, lined with cherry trees—a gift from Japan for the academy's anniversary in 2018. He had been deputy head of the Propulsion Department then, and at the welcoming dinner he had raised a glass with a retired technical officer from the Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force. The Japanese officer said that both countries' submarine propellers tended to be too large and too heavy: a problem they shared. Lu Weiming said, “Our materials aren't as good as yours. No amount of common ground makes up for that.”

The Japanese officer said nothing. Neither did Lu Weiming.

———

1995. Qingdao.

Lu Weiming was twenty-four. After completing his graduate studies in marine propulsion at Harbin Engineering University, he was assigned to the Naval Submarine Academy as a teaching assistant in the propulsion teaching and research section. Back then, he believed. Not in the manner of a soldier shouting slogans—he was not a soldier, but civilian staff. His belief was quieter: he believed that given the right materials, the right manufacturing processes, and enough time, Chinese submarines would one day no longer have to be louder underwater than the Americans'.

The section head, Meng, was a retired naval captain in his fifties. Years of service on nuclear submarines had damaged the hearing in his right ear—the noise inside a nuclear submarine was overwhelming. When he spoke, he always turned his left side toward you, presenting his good ear.

“Young Lu, you studied at Harbin Engineering. Let me ask you something.”

“Please, Director.”

“Why are Chinese submarine propellers always noisier than American ones?”

“Our CNC machining isn't precise enough on the curved blade surfaces. Germany's five-axis machine tools are under embargo, and the Japanese won't dare sell us even their used ones. Our domestic machine tools can manage seven axes, but their precision is off by about 0.02 millimeters—”

“Zero point zero two.”

“Yes.”

“Zero point zero two millimeters. That tiny amount makes our submarines fifteen decibels louder on sonar than the Americans'.”

“Yes.”

Director Meng turned his good ear away and looked out of the window for a while. “Young Lu, if you can turn that 0.02 into 0.01 in your lifetime, you will have done right by this country. Even if it is the only thing you ever accomplish.”

In his first five years at the academy, Lu Weiming made a decision: he would never develop a proposal that required the words “assuming domestic materials reach the same standard as imports.” Every design he produced was based on the best materials and equipment that could actually be purchased in China—which meant that from the outset, his designs were inferior to the Germans', the Japanese', and the ones the Americans sold to Australia. This was not for lack of effort. Across an entire industrial system, a gap of 0.02 millimeters was a wall.

He accepted that wall. What he could not accept was something else: at every review meeting, someone from another research group would have written “domestically produced XX has reached an internationally advanced standard” in their proposal. Sometimes Lu Weiming knew all too well what that standard really was—he kept up with the open literature. The latest American generation of seven-bladed, highly skewed propellers was a full generation ahead of ours in noise reduction. It wasn't that we couldn't catch up; we hadn't caught up yet. But you could not say “we haven't caught up yet” at a review meeting. You had to say “we are approaching their level.” Between “approaching” and “already reached,” there was only a flick of the report writer's pen.

He never made that flick of the pen. It left him something of an outsider at the institute.

———

2008. Qingdao.

Lu Weiming was thirty-seven, an associate professor. He married Shen Yu, a lecturer at the academy. Shen Yu taught political theory, but her research interest was the history of science education in modern China. She had spent years in archives, leafing through the notebooks of Soviet experts who had assisted China in the 1950s. When those experts left, they left their notebooks behind. Some passages were written in pencil; the paper had become brittle.

Once, she translated a passage for Lu Weiming. It came from a draft report a Soviet marine propulsion specialist had written to Moscow in 1958. It was never sent: Sino-Soviet relations had fractured that autumn.

“The Chinese learn very quickly. But they are trapped by a problem I cannot quite describe—they dare not tell us which parts they do not understand. At meetings they unanimously approve everything, saying, ‘We understand completely.’ Then we go to the workshop and discover that they have misunderstood the drawings. It is not that they are stupid. They have been told that they ‘must understand.’ You must understand, so we come; they say they understand, so we leave; the drawings are forever a little off.”

After listening, Lu Weiming was silent for a long time.

Shen Yu said, “It's the same thing you deal with every day.”

“It's different,” he said. “It isn't that we ‘dare not admit we don't understand.’ It's that we ‘cannot say what you've given us isn't good enough.’ If you say there's a problem with Soviet equipment, that's a political issue—the Soviet Union is our elder brother. If you say a domestic substitute isn't up to standard, that's a political issue too—domestic substitution is the Central Military Commission's strategy. So all we can say is ‘improvements are underway.’”

“Then what do you do every day?”

“In the cracks between ‘improvements are underway,’ I make real improvements.”

———

2021. Qingdao.

Lu Weiming was fifty, a full professor and head of the Propulsion Department. Their son, Lu Xiao, was in his third year of university in Beijing, studying journalism. Lu Xiao rarely came home—not because they were on bad terms, but because he was busy. Lu Weiming knew what his son was doing. Not from phone calls, but from publicly available reports online.

That year, Lu Xiao was summoned by the university for helping edit an independent student publication. No expulsion, no disciplinary action: “ideological education.” Lu Weiming and Shen Yu went to Beijing to see him. Lu Xiao had lost a lot of weight, but he was not dispirited. The first thing he said when he saw his parents was, “Dad, I didn't do anything wrong.”

“I know. You didn't do anything wrong.”

They went out for lamb hotpot. Lu Xiao talked the whole time—about the people he had interviewed who had been displaced by demolition, about which regulation had been invoked to classify their magazine as an “illegal publication,” about the archives they had searched. Shen Yu listened. Lu Weiming listened too, but at the same time he was thinking: in the eyes of the system, what his son was doing and what he did at the Submarine Academy were the same thing. Both had been offered a choice between what was good and what existed, and had chosen the former. The only difference was that his battlefield was a propeller with a tolerance of 0.02 millimeters, while his son's was a fact-finding review that refused to acknowledge that some regulations were unreasonable.

When he walked Lu Xiao back to his dormitory, he stood outside for a while.

“Do you know what your grandfather thought when I went into propulsion engineering? He said, ‘So you're learning to make those things?’ I said yes. He said, ‘Then you'll have plenty to keep you busy for the rest of your life.’ Your grandfather asked me much the same question, once. That was all he asked.”

Lu Xiao looked at the streetlight. “I've never considered not being a journalist.”

“Good. Be a good one.”

“How are your propellers coming along?”

“Ten years ago, the gap was 0.015. Now it's 0.01. There's still 0.01 to go.”

“So when you catch up, you've won.”

“No. Catching up will mean the country can make good propellers. It won't mean anything else. Propellers aren't the only thing we're behind on.”

It was the first time he had said that to his son. Lu Xiao did not answer; he simply looked at him. Under the streetlight, a father and son who would not lie to each other stood outside the dormitory in the Beijing winter night. Lu Xiao tugged at his scarf. “Go inside, Dad. It's cold out. Next time, tell me how you first decided you didn't want to be a cog in the machine.”

Lu Weiming did not answer. He went home and slept badly that night. He remembered the Soviet notebook Shen Yu had read to him. “They dare not tell us which parts they do not understand.” That line was no longer pencil on paper. It was his son standing alone in the wind at the stairwell entrance.

———

2031. Qingdao.

Lu Weiming was sixty. Lu Xiao was in prison. A two-year sentence, for an offense written into a newly amended security provision. Shen Yu wrote one letter every week; none was ever delivered. She folded each into a small triangle and put it in her desk drawer, saying she would keep them safe on their behalf for now.

Every week, Lu Weiming went to the academy's anechoic tank laboratory to continue testing propeller noise. Seven blades, highly skewed, CNC-machined on domestically produced five-axis machine tools. The latest results: two decibels quieter than the equivalent American generation.

Two decibels.

He stood alone beside the anechoic tank, looking at the wall lights reflected on the water. Forty years: 0.02 millimeters had become minus two decibels. They had surpassed them. Surpassed them. He ought to have been happy. All his students sent congratulations: Professor Lu, you did it. If only Director Meng were still alive. The old director had died of lung cancer twenty years earlier. Who could say whether the noise in his right ear had come from the submarine or from the world?

Lu Weiming wept beside the tank.

Not from happiness, and not from sadness. It was because the end of that 0.02 was so quiet. There was not even a celebration—the entire navy was busy with other things. The situation in the south. Whether the defense budget should be cut or increased. Who would rise to the top in next year's leadership transition.

A nuclear submarine, the quietest propeller in the world, and his son behind another wall. The same wall no longer had room for two kinds of people. One kind brought noise levels below the Americans'. Another put his son behind a wall.

———

June 2042. Qingdao.

Six months after the transitional government was formed, Lu Xiao was released. He was among the first political prisoners to be exonerated. Lu Weiming went to collect him and realized that his own hair had turned completely white, and Lu Xiao's nearly so. Prison had aged a man fifteen years in five.

Lu Xiao hugged Shen Yu first. Then he stepped back and looked at Lu Weiming.

“Dad, your propellers caught up.”

“They did. The year you went to prison. Two decibels quieter than the Americans'.”

“Two decibels. Was it worth it?”

Lu Weiming did not answer then. That evening they ate at home. Shen Yu made the sweet-and-sour ribs Lu Xiao had loved as a boy. As they washed up, Lu Xiao took a bowl from his father's hands. “You still haven't answered me.”

“There's no question of whether it was worth it. I did one thing, and you did another. Neither of us did anything wrong.”

“It's different. What you did was right. Whatever that system was like. What you did was right.”

“What you did was right too.”

“I was wrong. Not to publish the magazine. To think I could change anything.”

Lu Weiming set the bowl beside the sink and looked at Lu Xiao. He wanted to tell him: you didn't think that at twenty-two, under the streetlight. At twenty-two, you stood in the wind and told me you hadn't done anything wrong. Back then, you thought you could change things. They changed you. You didn't change them.

He wanted to say it. But he didn't.

He only took his son's bowl and rinsed it again.

———

December 2043. Qingdao.

The present. The slogan at the Submarine Academy had been taken down. Nobody knew what would replace it. On retiring, Lu Weiming received a new pension assessment form—not the red kind, but a printout of an electronic document, with no five-pointed star in the heading, using the new Ministry of Maritime and Defense Affairs' standard format.

He handed in his staff ID, his laboratory access card, and his security clearance certificate. The administrator put them in a kraft-paper envelope and said, “Professor Lu, is there anything else you need taken care of that I can help you with?”

He said no. Then he said:

“I worked on improvements to four propeller models in all. For two of them, the final test data in the files are a little conservative. I recalculated them recently—”

“I'm sorry, Professor Lu, but the system won't let me retrieve those files at the moment. They're governed by the new secrecy regulations—”

“That's enough. Leave them.”

He stood at the campus gate for a long time. The ginkgo tree he had known since arriving in 1987 was still there, bare. Qingdao's winter sea wind blew in, mixing the smell of salt with diesel. In the naval harbor, the ships were being repainted—one by one, “Chinese People's Liberation Army Navy” was becoming “Chinese Navy.” Flags came down, new flags went up. It was not a dynastic change, not a revolution, not one of those great days described in history books. It was something subtler—an old name exchanged for a new one with a word missing.

At a press conference the previous month, the transitional government's defense minister had said: “The Chinese Navy will no longer be the armed force of any political party, but the navy of all the Chinese people.” When the academy's political commissar heard those words, he was filling out the form for his transfer to a civilian post. He worked on it for an hour, then put down his pen and went for a cigarette.

Lu Weiming walked home. Shen Yu was making fish soup in the kitchen. The radio was playing the news: public consultation had begun on the new constitutional amendments. Lu Xiao was asleep on the living-room sofa—he was writing for a newly established independent news agency and had worked through three nights in a row.

Lu Weiming sat at the dining table and took something from his pocket.

A workshop key. A label was taped to it, in his handwriting: “Anechoic Tank No. 7.”

He sat holding the key for a long time. Years ago, when he had last entered the tank room, there had been no one within its four walls, and every sound wave sank beneath the water. What he heard was not the pump, not the bearings, not the blades he had revised fifty times. He heard the words his son had spoken in the wind.

“Next time, tell me how you first decided you didn't want to be a cog in the machine.”

He left the key on the dining table. The fish soup had come to a boil. Shen Yu was calling him.

A key could not unlock a wind. But when the wind stopped, he was still there. He was not a cog. He was a wedge that had chosen its own wood—forty years spent driven into the least conspicuous crack in that wall. The wall had not fallen. But it was cracking, and a little wind was pouring through.

He picked up the key, set it down again, and went to Shen Yu.

———

(The End)
