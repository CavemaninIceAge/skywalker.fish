—Forty-seven years in the life of a Crimean Tatar

December 25, 1991. A hillside near Bakhchysarai.

Refat Umerov, fifty-five, was hanging a door. He had made it himself, out of pine, planed it yesterday, and was fitting the hinges today. There was no electricity on the hillside. A battery-powered radio stood on the windowsill. It said that at 7:32 p.m. Moscow time, the red flag over the Kremlin had been lowered. He did not stop working. The hinges had to line up. If they did not, the door would not close, and if it did not close, they would not make it through the winter.

When the door was hung, he took a key from his pocket, iron, old-fashioned, its teeth worn round. He hammered a nail beside the doorframe and hung the key on it.

The key could not open this door. The door had no lock, either.

———

May 18, 1944. Crimea.

Refat was eight. At four in the morning somebody pounded on the door. Soldiers. They said fifteen minutes. His mother Esma dressed, filled a sack with flour, put the Quran into the flour, and pushed him and his younger sister out of the door. Once outside, she turned and locked it, tucking the key into her clothes as though they were going out for the day.

Everyone in the village was gathered in front of the mosque. Trucks came, took them to the station, and loaded them into freight cars. Sixty people to a car. The doors were locked from outside. They traveled for eighteen days. His sister, Ayshe, was four. She died on the twelfth day. Her body remained in the car for two days. When the train stopped, the soldiers told them to carry her out and bury her there. The train would not wait. The burial place had no name. His mother remembered the name of the station and never forgot it.

It was June when they reached Uzbekistan. Cotton fields. Special settlers. They had to report to the military commandant’s office every month and obtain permission to leave the district. He was eight. He had to report too.

The Uzbeks in the village had been told beforehand that traitors were coming, with horns on their heads. What they saw was a freight car full of women and children. On the first evening an old Uzbek woman passed a flatbread through the fence. His mother took it and said thank you, in Russian, because that was the only word they shared.

His mother hung the key on the wall of their mud-brick house. She did not say what it was. It just hung there.

———

November 1945. Uzbekistan.

His father came back. He had been at the front since 1941 and fought his way to Berlin. He wore two decorations on his chest: an Order of the Red Star and a Medal for the Capture of Berlin. It took him six months to find them. He went to Crimea first. Other people lived in their village. He asked, Where is my family? The others said, We don’t know. They left. The district officials said, Look in Uzbekistan. He spent a month on trains, asking from district to district. On the day he found them, he stood at the door of the mud-brick house, looked at the key on the wall, and said nothing.

The next day he reported to the commandant’s office. A man who had fought his way to Berlin had to report every month in a district of Uzbekistan and was forbidden to leave. His medals were no use. He was a Crimean Tatar, and Crimean Tatars were traitors. That had been decided in 1944, while he was outside Berlin.

His father never uttered a word of complaint in his life. He simply did not eat on May 18 each year, for one day. He did not explain. The whole family fasted with him.

———

1956. Uzbekistan.

The restrictions were lifted. The sign came down from the commandant’s office; the reporting ledger was taken away. His father went to the district office and asked: Can we go back now? The official said, You can go anywhere. His father said, What about Crimea? The official said, Anywhere except there.

His father came home, repeated the sentence to the family exactly, then went to the cotton fields. Refat was twenty, and for the first time understood that they were not being locked up. They were being left somewhere. Left here, forbidden to return there. People left somewhere had no walls around them, so nobody thought they were unfree.

———

1968. Crimea.

In 1956 the restrictions had been lifted. They no longer had to report, but they were not allowed to return to Crimea. In 1967 the authorities issued a document saying that the accusations of 1944 no longer stood, but it did not say they could return. Refat was thirty-two. After reading it, he decided that the people writing the document were the same people who would not let them go home, so the document could not count.

He went back. With ten years of savings he bought a house near Bakhchysarai. The seller was Ukrainian. He signed the papers and took the money. A month later a court declared the sale invalid because Refat had no residence registration. The police put him and his luggage on a train. He bought the ticket himself. The officer escorting him was young, a local, and did not look at him the whole way. On the platform he said one thing: Uncle, don’t come again. Next time it won’t just be a fine. On the train Refat did the sums: the price of the house was unrecoverable; travel had cost 140 rubles, plus the fine. He was thirty-two and earned 110 rubles a month.

He wrote the figures in a notebook. He wrote nothing else. There was only that one page. Over the next twenty years he never made another entry. He thought one was enough, enough to remember what this was.

His father died in Uzbekistan in 1971. Before he died, he took the key down from the wall and handed it to Refat. He said nothing. This family did not speak much.

———

July 1987. Moscow.

On July 23, several hundred Crimean Tatars sat down in Red Square. They had spent three days on trains from Uzbekistan. Refat, fifty-one, was among them. They sat in front of Lenin’s Mausoleum holding sheets of paper with words on them. The police surrounded them but did not intervene. Foreign reporters took photographs. The papers bore three words: Homeland, or, Death. A Moscow woman passing by looked for a long time, then asked someone beside her: What do they want? Don’t they have Uzbekistan? More people came the next day. More on the third.

On the third evening a man came out of the Kremlin to speak to them. He said a commission would be set up to study the question, and asked everyone to go home.

Refat went back. He felt he had been hearing the word “study” for forty-three years. But this time reporters had taken photographs. He thought things that had been photographed might be different from things that had not.

In November 1989 the Supreme Soviet adopted a declaration stating that the deportation of 1944 had been illegal, a crime. Refat read it in a newspaper in Uzbekistan and cut it out. The declaration said their rights would be guaranteed, but did not say how. This time, however, it did not say they were forbidden.

———

April 1990. Crimea.

He returned. Fifty-four. This time he did not buy a house. He could not afford one, and nobody dared sell him one anyway.

First he went to the village. It was still there. Its name had changed. His birthplace had had a Tatar name. In 1948 it had been given a Russian one meaning “bright.” His family’s house was still there too, white, with a replaced roof. A woman of about sixty was hanging laundry in the yard. He stood outside the fence. The woman asked whom he was looking for. He said, This is my home. She said, This is my home. I came from Voronezh in 1946. I’ve lived here forty-four years. He said, I lived here eight.

Neither of them was lying.

He did not go in. He looked at the door. It had been replaced with an iron one. The key in his pocket could not open it. He knew that.

He went up the hillside behind the village. More than twenty households were already there, returnees from Uzbekistan, living in shacks of roofing felt and wooden boards. The government called it an unauthorized land occupation, illegal. They said, We don’t call it occupying. We call it coming back.

On the hillside were two doctors, a secondary school teacher, an engineer, and a tractor driver, all building walls. In the evenings they met in a shack to discuss where to gather stones and where to carry water from. The water came from the village well. The villagers neither stopped them nor helped. One morning a bucket stood beside the fence, full. The woman in his family’s yard had left it there. She did not show herself. He poured the water into his own bucket and returned hers to the fence. They still had not spoken a second time.

In May bulldozers came and flattened everything. In June they built again. In August the bulldozers came again. In September they built again. The bulldozers did not come a third time. Not because permission had been granted. Because the district had no diesel.

———

December 25, 1991.

Now. Now the door was hung, and the key hung beside it.

His mother Esma, seventy-eight, sat by the stove. He had carried her up the hillside on his back the year before. She looked at the key for a long time.

“Where’s the lock?”

“There is no lock.”

“Good.”

He and his son had built the house together. Three rooms, stone walls, stones gathered on the mountain, forty stones laid a day for a year. The roof went on in October. No electricity, no residence registration, no title deed. District officials had come once, looked around, said this was an unauthorized land occupation, and left. They had not returned. They were busy with other things. Too much had happened this year for them to bother with a hillside.

The first winter on the hillside: more than twenty families, eleven stoves, firewood from the mountain. The district said they were not allowed to cut the trees. They cut them. The district did not come.

The radio was speaking Ukrainian. He switched it off.

His son asked what the key was doing there.

He said, Hanging.

The door was new; the key was old. They did not match. He looked at them for a while and thought that was good.

The thing he had waited for all his life was not the door that this key could open. That door had been replaced in 1946, by an iron one. He had waited for the day he could hang the key beside a door, and that door would be in Crimea. Today he had hung it there.

———

March 1992. Simferopol.

His grandson was born in a hospital in Simferopol. His son came back from the hospital and placed the birth certificate on the table.

He put on his glasses and read the birthplace field. Simferopol, Crimea.

The Umerov family’s first piece of paper in forty-eight years that said Crimea. The earlier ones bore place names in Uzbekistan: his father’s death certificate, his mother’s. For his sister there had not even been a piece of paper.

He looked at the birth certificate for a long time. His mother asked him to read out the birthplace. He did. She nodded and closed her eyes. She had been thirty-one when she locked the door in 1944. Now she was seventy-nine. She said nothing. This family did not speak much.

He gave the birth certificate back to his son. He did not hang it beside the key. It was not something to be hung up. It was something to be used.

(The End)
