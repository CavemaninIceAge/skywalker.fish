Late at night I came across old photographs and suddenly felt terribly sad. They were still from my rented room. It was never merely a place to stay. It was the life I had lived for the past three years…

Tonight I remember every piece of furniture there and exactly where it stood, with limitless clarity. I know where the tiles were stained reddish brown the day I mopped with potassium permanganate. I remember the colors and shadows of everything when afternoon sunlight reached the windowsill. Speaking of the windowsill, for a long time before the college entrance exam I had stopped climbing through the window onto the little balcony outside it. I remember viewing the place on a bright morning. We looked at only two places; this was the first, and at first sight I knew I wanted to live there. I remember the clear sunlight filling the room that morning. On the windowsill was a little dog riding a bicycle and pulling three balloons, and beyond it the open little balcony I had always wanted. As we went upstairs, three white cats with green eyes sat at the second-floor turn. I had already begun dreaming of a life where I would see white cats every morning and evening. Because their eyes were so green, I remember distinctly that the dragon relief on the outside wall of No. 3 High School was painted green too, a rougher, darker green than the cats’ eyes, darker even than verdigris.

I never saw those three cats again. But for three summers I watched workers repair the heating pipes. In the warm weather the earth was dug up, and the family on the first floor would let more kittens out. They leapt lightly over the dirt and red-brick walls without a speck of dust catching in their white fur. On my second day of school, Dad was staying there during the day with the door open, and a kitten walked in, meowing. Dad stood in the doorway, blocking its path, and eventually it left. Later I saw cats in spring, saw them while running a one-kilometer circuit of the neighborhood in summer, saw them in autumn, and heard them one winter while lying on the roof in the stillness of the night. Every close friend of mine meowed at the cats outside with me. Every close friend came to my home many times.

The room held my impressions of all my friends. Your shadows lingered on every piece of furniture, some from my first year of high school heavy, some from later years light. On the fourth day of the Lunar New Year in early 2022, after a lonely, dispiriting New Year’s Eve, a friend came over after ten at night. He taught me Chinese chess until two in the morning. Then we headed north through wind and snow to watch the sunrise over the river. We never saw the sun that day. Jingyang Street was empty, with only our voices and the sound of our feet in the snow. When we returned after five, it was getting light. I watched my friend fall asleep on the sofa as daylight arrived. Later he came to lie on my bed. During countless weekends before May in the second semester of my first year, someone else came over day after day. In the mornings we usually studied; in the afternoons we went everywhere in Harbin we could reach by bicycle. Once we went to the Ashi River and parted in the evening on the way back. The cat still lived with me then. When he was there, it disappeared. As soon as he left, it jumped down from the tall wardrobe.

I put so many things on that little table, some about myself, some about us. I still remember the days before I had internet, when I went downstairs every evening to borrow the wonton shop’s Wi-Fi to submit homework. I remember every one of those days.

My little rented room was not simply a thin point in space and time. It radiated into everything that came and went. I still remember all the shops and all the people in the neighborhood, and they all knew me too. I loved a life where I had to exchange several greetings over the last two hundred meters home before I could get through the door. I knew every one of them, though not their names. I knew the woman who ran the fruit shop downstairs was kind and that her son was at university. The bookshop owner was a little afraid of his wife, sometimes endearingly absent-minded. I also knew all the stories of all the students the convenience-shop owner had known over twenty years. Perhaps the most moving thing was the beef-noodle shop owner’s son. When I arrived, he was little more than a name. When I visited one noon after my college entrance exam, the child could already walk and was laughing happily outside with his grandmother.

From there I set out for Alejin Island in the river during the pandemic, for my parents’ home, for the little internet café on Tongjiang Street, for the Grand Theatre. I saw wind, frost, rain, snow, and clear skies… It was naturally the Rome of my life. I went out from it and brought everything back: bouquets I had arranged, kites and balloon frogs bought by the river, unleavened bread from church. It was the root of my life, a thing-in-itself that would never collapse, even when my life came infinitely close to collapse several times. But one day that root was pulled up. All the life I had laid down over three years rose into the air like blades of grass scattered high into the sky.

That is what happened today. I once packed a box of traces from my life there, but they were merely inert material. When I suddenly opened the old photographs, a vitality that perhaps can never be packed away came back to life. Everything from the past flowed abundantly before my eyes. For the first time I discovered that I could not do without that life. I missed every moment that had passed, and those moments had already passed into the past, like some kind of black iron.

I would never claim that high school was perfectly happy or unbearably hard to leave. I have to admit that much of the time I probably grew through inner turmoil. Still, from the medals hanging on my clothes to the calendar and pictures on the wall, the little lucky stars I arranged for myself on the table, and the beaker I used to heat milk, everything bore witness to the efforts I made to “find a life” inside my isolation. My exercise books finally stacked up to one and a half times my height. I saw the moon caught between buildings on my walk back from the river and always heard the rush of trains on the tracks at night. Perhaps nothing was very glamorous. But that slight young person, working to live and grow, would always begin again with vitality in the new morning light. Without him then, I would not be here now. He has been left behind in that time, and the journey has become an everlasting remembrance.

Nostalgia is always an aesthetics of loss, and perhaps “the past” becomes worth remembering precisely because it is lost. Those lives seem beyond reach, yet so many essential things have continued to this day. Old experiences and new challenges together have shaped the way I now see the world. Looking closely at them, I find a way to understand life…
