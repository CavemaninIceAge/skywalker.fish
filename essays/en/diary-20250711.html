I remember coming across a piece of writing that touched my soul in the early hours of my eighteenth birthday:

“A few days ago, a beloved teacher from my university years called and said he wanted to visit my hometown and see my parents. After we met and exchanged greetings, I took him along the country paths once traveled by a boy who tended horses.

It was a stretch of woodland spanning both banks of the river that nourished this place, lush with water plants, where cattle and horses lingered. These days, one bank has been occupied by the ‘Manchu Tribe’ riding grounds and turned into a rustic leisure spot for barbecues and scenic views. As a child tending horses, I often lay in the woods along the great river beside the road, bored out of my mind, reading Romance of the Three Kingdoms on a sack spread on the ground. ‘When I put the book aside, my heart found nowhere to rest.’ Yet in the woodland breeze and the dappled sunlight, I would always drift listlessly to sleep. Day after day, without end. Time stretched so long it made you drowsy.

Sometimes I caught the ‘fragrance’ of car exhaust carried on the wind and thought: this is what the city smells like. Listening to cars rush past the roadside, I felt as if I alone had been forgotten by the whole world. It was an absence of ‘meaning.’ Even the air was so clean it bored me.

Where I was, time stood still. Farming year after year could bring only despair, because you had no other choice. Returning home now, the pleasant breeze, the stars filling the sky, the frogs in the pond, and the village roosters naturally soothe the soul. They make the world feel vast and life feel simple again. But thirty years ago, all of that could bring a kind of despair to a young and restless heart. In your elders bent ninety degrees toward the yellow earth, you could glimpse your own future. What pastoral idyll? ‘Farmhouse leisure’ is an urban affliction. My parents, who lived there, never found anything leisurely about farm life. So-called hometown nostalgia is merely the photo filter of those who have left, compensation for a sense of loss. All the cheerful recollections afterward amount to making peace with the self who lived through those years without meaning. But that is afterward. Not everyone gets an afterward.

I very nearly didn’t. Born in the early 1980s, I was allowed into this world only after an entrance fee of 1,600 yuan had been paid. I always wanted to know how much 1,600 yuan back then would be worth today, and what it really meant. Later, I asked my university teacher. He told me that when he first started working in those years, his monthly salary was 46 yuan.

I asked my mother too. She said that working on the production team, she earned five to eight work points a day, equivalent to fifty to eighty fen. I pressed her: what could one work point buy? She said half a kilogram of coarse salt mixed with mud and sand. Whenever I open WeChat and see the tiny figure facing a blue planet, I always feel that figure is me, and the blue planet confronting me is nothing more than eight thousand kilograms of sea salt compressed into a sphere. Later, when I told my students, they teased me: ‘Professor, they’re encouraging people to have more children now. Can you get the 1,600 yuan back?’ I could only smile bitterly.

Walking through the village today, I seem to be treated with respect. But when I was little, the nickname ‘Sixteen Hundred’ was often brought up as a joke. There is no need to deny that it left some unpleasant gray marks on a child’s heart.

My father had been the village public-security officer. While my mother, several months pregnant, lived in fear and endured every hardship dodging the family-planning team’s pursuit, he had gone all the way to Changchun to catch a donkey thief. Even when he later took me to Changchun to start university, he still boasted with youthful swagger about how cleverly and bravely he had caught that petty criminal. He never mentioned that he had lost his job after I was born, nor any of the hardships our family had faced afterward.

In the village, he was known as an honest man. A high-school graduate, he had lost his chance to attend university because of that particular era. Though he was a talented young man, as the eldest son he chose to remain beside his parents to care for my grandfather, whose legs were bad. When I asked about all the tragicomic events surrounding my birth, my mother always reminded me: so-and-so lent our family this much money; we mustn’t forget that kindness. Without so-and-so, there would be no you; you owe them a debt of gratitude. My father, by contrast, chose silence. He seemed to have quietly accepted the arrangements of fate, as though a device had been installed in his head, leaving him without joy or sorrow. On the rare occasion I asked, he would look at the sky outside the window, think for a moment, and then smile at me: ‘I don’t remember.’ Sometimes a teacher, friend, or fellow villager would mention that someone from his old class had prospered and become a district Party secretary or the like. He would just smile and say, ‘Oh.’ Gradually, father and son stopped talking about ‘back then.’ It was like a double wall of memory. He did not want to bring it up; I no longer wanted to ask. In our shared silence, we both knew what it meant, and carefully fitted a pair of shoes onto the memories that had been trampling us barefoot.

I often quote an ancient philosopher to admonish my students solemnly: the unexamined life is not worth living. But I know what weight and cruelty those memories hold for my father. Four years ago, he was in a car accident. Although he survived, the early signs of dementia he had already shown gave way to increasing confusion. To keep his limbs moving, one of the daily exercises my mother assigned him was writing—his literary ability and calligraphy had something of a reputation in the village. So I teased him: ‘Why don’t you write about when I was little?’ He really did fill two pages in a shaky hand, then told me, ‘Send me one of your books to copy instead.’ I know he wants to live in a stretch of time he has cut out for himself, where he can smile without first having to pause and think.

For my mother, however, the past has never faded like smoke. Although she seldom brings up old stories herself, I have known since childhood that she has always held a fierce resolve inside. I know that I am the wager she made with this world, and I am willing to sit at the table with her—as both gambler and stake.

Years later, on visits home, I sometimes happen to be there for the town’s Wednesday market. It is not only a place to trade, but a place to socialize. My mother is not someone who likes to show off. Yet whenever she runs into people who once nearly prevented my ‘debut’ in this world, she takes me over and reminisces with them until their faces show embarrassment. I know she is not flaunting the present. She is solemnly announcing the outcome of that long gamble.

Fortunately, I lived in a time when even a boy tending horses could stake everything on a chance. Years later, when people asked why I loved reading so much, I could only smile bitterly: what else was there? I teach at a top university in the 985 Project, and in recent years I have often represented my school in undergraduate admissions in one of China’s centrally administered municipalities. Then it struck me: most candidates who reached the first-tier admission cutoff came from the city’s four prestigious high schools, and getting into those schools depended in turn on owning a home in the right school district. That meant students at elite universities were increasingly drawn from wealthy families.

Once, a student from an ordinary high school in an outlying county, whose score was hovering around our estimated cutoff, came to the admissions team’s city base with his principal, homeroom teacher, and parents. Everything about him, from his complexion to the look in his eyes, reminded me of that boy tending horses in the woods. I closed the door to other visitors and spent nearly an hour discussing things with them in detail. It was easy to see in his parents’ eyes that he was this family’s only stake in its wager with the world. But the bridge the times had left them toward an ‘afterward’ was growing narrower. Perhaps the only difference was my own expensive entrance fee. As a child, I often asked myself: how am I going to pay this money back? In an age without ‘meaning,’ this became my first practical source of it. All I could think of was earning more and spending less. First, I consumed almost nothing beyond the essentials of food, sleep, and school. I even refused new clothes my parents bought me. Second, I combed the hills and fields for discarded bottles and scrap iron. Anything that could be exchanged for money was worth looking for. I pushed it all in a handcart to the scrap dealer, then brought the money home to my mother. Dinner always tasted especially good that night. After all, for several years my older sister used to teasingly remind me that, as the family’s unregistered child, I had no allotment of land and could eat a little less.

In life, not only can you choose neither the manner of your entrance nor its price; even the first half of your life is constrained by so many preconditions. My father’s acceptance, my mother’s forbearance, my own guilt—all shaped successive versions of ‘me,’ day after day. And the person we must eventually face is that small childhood self. For many, then, ‘leaving childhood behind’ becomes a lifelong, unbearable weight.

Most arguments on the subway break out among people without seats. We often roll our eyes at their noise, easily forgetting that if they had seats, none of it would have happened. Twenty years ago, on the day I arrived in Shanghai, I emerged from a crowded subway train to find my aunt’s son waiting for me. Born in a remote mountain village, he had by then risen to a senior position at one of the Big Four accounting firms. For a long time, he was an idol far beyond my reach. When I asked what life had taught him since leaving home, he said quietly: ‘Don’t complain. Don’t lament. Live your way out of the bitterness your family left you.’ I was startled, then smiled again—I who had never complained or lamented—and raised my glass to drink with him, thanking him for those words: live your way out. For the first time, I realized that life could be an art of possibility, and that I could become an ‘artist’ beyond the jurisdiction of adjectives.

Only after turning it over in my mind did I gradually understand what had always moved me about my cousin: the strength of seeing himself as a survivor, not a victim, even though he had not always been strong. With no one to hold an umbrella over him, he could only run as hard as he could. From the moment you resolve to ‘live your way out,’ your opponent is time. It compresses your past and present, and determines your ‘afterward.’

Today, deliveries and internet connections have long since reached my hometown. Rural life is increasingly absorbed into the rhythms of the city, becoming their extension. A few years ago, enormous wind turbines appeared on the distant hills. Their three blades turn ceaselessly in the wind, like hands that have lost their clock faces, like Mercedes stars stripped of their rings. They seem a vast metaphor: between sunrise and sunset, something is being accelerated.

The pumping station—the waterworks—a kilometer from our home now supplies the urban area’s drinking water. Yet my family still uses well water. As poultry farms have multiplied, the groundwater has become polluted, and many villagers suffer strange illnesses. Each time my sister and I return, we can only buy stacks of bottled water from the shop to cover our parents’ daily needs for several months. On the walk to buy water, scarcely anyone is in sight. The women, children, and elderly people remaining in the village seem no longer to be bored either. Douyin and Kuaishou playing aloud supply the ‘meaning’ they need. In this world of sound and images that asks nothing of the intellect, infinite distant places and countless people all seem connected to them. ‘Far away’ no longer requires imagination; it is right before their eyes. Thirty years ago, we waded in to catch fish, climbed the hills to work, held pinwheels aloft, and ran like the wind. Amid boredom and hard labor, we tried to discover and create life’s ‘meaning,’ imagining distant places. Most villagers no longer want to look back, as though poverty were all the past contained. Rural life in the eighties and nineties was certainly hard and rough, with the good and bad all mixed together. Yet its exuberant vitality cannot be denied. Imperceptibly, an era is receding, and something else is withering with it.

Back home, I lie on the heated brick bed and eat the old-fashioned sponge cake I never let myself have enough of as a child because I felt guilty toward my parents. I watch the swallows building nests under the eaves outside the window. Sometimes I leap into the muddy river for a wash, just as I used to. When villagers call me ‘Sixteen Hundred’ on the road, I take pleasure in it—there is no need to return home in glory. I want to go back to the grass among the trees, look at the sky amid the smell of horse dung and the sound of cicadas, and recover the person I forgot, even abandoned, in my effort to ‘live my way out.’ He is the clay beneath the glaze, the original self from before I stepped off the subway and began that life. So let the professor return to those woods and find ‘Sixteen Hundred.’ Let him lie beside the boy on a sack, looking at the sky, turn to ruffle his hair, and ask: ‘Hey, buddy. What’s on your mind?’

Nostalgia is an aesthetics of loss, and perhaps a ‘hometown’ is something made to be lost—in reality, and in our field of vision. I cannot put a frame around the wind turbines on the distant hills. But as long as you do not feel contempt or fear, ‘Sixteen Hundred’ can still be found. Do not forget: the ‘Sixteen Hundred of the woods’ and the ‘professor of the academy,’ from before and after you ‘lived your way out,’ together shape your way of seeing the world. And by looking closely at them, you find a way to understand this land.”

I don’t want to add much. The words above express my own outlook and state of mind. Praise be to literature.

I want to say this to myself too: “Do not forget: the ‘Sixteen Hundred of the woods’ and the ‘professor of the academy,’ from before and after you ‘lived your way out,’ together shape your way of seeing the world. And by looking closely at them, you find a way to understand this land.”

Feel neither contempt nor fear. Those times and the present together make up my way of understanding this land. All of it is my life.
