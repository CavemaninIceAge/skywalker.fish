—The last day of a Soviet nuclear submarine sonar officer

December 25, 1991. The Soviet Union dissolved.

Sergei Nikolayevich Belov, forty-four, a captain first rank in the Soviet Navy’s Northern Fleet, sat in the command compartment of a Project 667B nuclear submarine at the naval port of Severomorsk. He was not on duty. His submarine, K-219, had been removed from the order of battle three months earlier. Its hull still bore the dents from the 1987 accident off Bermuda. He had come today to collect his personal belongings.

In his locker was a fleet newspaper from 1980. Its front-page headline read: “The Red Banner Northern Fleet—the World’s Most Powerful Nuclear Submarine Force.” Beneath the newspaper lay an Order of the Red Banner, awarded for a mission tracking an American Ohio-class submarine in the Barents Sea in 1981. He had been a junior lieutenant then, a sonar officer, and had tracked a Lafayette-class submarine beneath the ice for seventy-two hours without losing contact. After they returned, the fleet commander decorated him in person. “Comrade Belov,” he said, “you are the finest ears in the Soviet Navy.”

He turned the medal over. On the back was engraved “1981.04.12.” Three days later he met his wife, Katya. It was a Thursday.

He put the medal into a shoebox and went on emptying the locker. The next thing was an unopened bottle of Armenian brandy, pressed into his hands by the executive officer when he was promoted to lieutenant commander in 1985. “Save it,” the executive officer had said. “We’ll drink it the day we sink the Americans.”

The executive officer had left three months ago to join a fishing company in Murmansk. He was now trawling for king crab in the Barents Sea. The Americans had not been sunk, and the executive officer’s liver was nearly done for.

———

1974. Sevastopol.

Sergei was twenty-seven, freshly graduated from the Frunze Naval Academy and assigned to the Black Sea Fleet. Back then he believed. Not the kind of belief that raises a hand at a Party congress, but something more personal: he believed in the sea. He believed in submarines. You were inside an iron tube with hundreds of meters of water above your head, all darkness and pressure; you needed something absolute in there to keep from going mad. He believed his country had given him that absolute.

On the day he first boarded a submarine, the captain was a veteran of the Cuban Missile Crisis. Pointing to a flag in the command compartment, the captain said, “We carried this flag to within twenty-one nautical miles of the Cuban coast. Twenty-one. American carrier aircraft were flying overhead, and their destroyers had our position marked perfectly on sonar. But they didn’t stop us, because we had nuclear warheads too. Do you understand? It wasn’t that we were strong. They wanted to find out whether we dared.”

“Did we?”

The captain looked at him. “We’re here, aren’t we?”

Sergei remembered that sentence for seventeen years. Only later did he realize that the captain had never actually answered him.

———

1982. The Barents Sea.

Sergei was thirty-five, a lieutenant commander, already a legendary sonar officer in the Northern Fleet. From thirty nautical miles away he could tell a Los Angeles-class submarine from three Ohio-class boats. This was no boast; he really could. The Americans had nicknamed him “the Ghost of the Barents,” a name his side later discovered in a declassified intercept.

But two things happened in 1982.

First: on a patrol, his sonar picked up a strange signal. It was not an American submarine, but a noise he had never heard before. He brought the recording back for intelligence to analyze. A month later the answer came: it was the propeller noise of a new submarine just launched from a Soviet northern shipyard. The boat was undergoing sea trials, and the propeller noise of the submarine he served on was three times as loud. In other words, he had spent ten years tracking Americans, but the real gap was in his own propeller.

A friend in intelligence told him privately that the Americans had fitted their submarines with “towed-array sonar” in 1981, with more than twice the detection range of Soviet sonar. The Soviet alternative was still on the drawing boards at Factory 404, because the factory had not received its shipment of imported high-precision bearings the year before. Bearings. They needed imported bearings to make sonar. A country that could build nuclear warheads could not make a high-precision bearing.

Second: his son Misha wrote from Leningrad saying he was listening to a band called Kino. He enclosed a short lyric: “Our hearts are waiting for change.” Sergei did not know what it was, but the words mentioned neither the Party nor Lenin nor anything that was supposed to appear in his son’s textbooks. He folded the letter and did not reply. He wanted to, but did not know what to say. You cannot answer your son’s loneliness with a fleet political department directive on “Guarding Against the Infiltration of Western Ideology.”

———

October 1987. Off Bermuda.

Sergei was forty, a commander. The nuclear submarine K-219 suffered a missile fuel leak roughly 680 nautical miles northeast of Bermuda. The oxidizer in an R-27 liquid-fueled missile was leaking, filling the submarine with highly toxic nitric acid fumes. Three sailors died on the spot while trying to stop the leak. The captain ordered an emergency surfacing.

Sergei was in the command compartment. He remembered one thing: when they broke the surface, an American Perry-class frigate was already three nautical miles away. Three nautical miles. The Americans had been following them, following them every minute they were underwater. All the way across the Atlantic, right up to the moment they surfaced.

The only offer of help came when the submarine surfaced and sailors began collapsing from poisoning. The American frigate sent an international plain-language message: “Do you require medical assistance?”

Did they require medical assistance. The people he had spent half his life pursuing were asking, as he was dying, whether he needed help. Every American submarine he had ever tracked, every American who had played hide-and-seek with him underwater, asked only this one question at that moment.

He signaled back: “We are abandoning ship. We do not require assistance.” Then he ordered the Soviet flag raised and all hands to abandon ship.

It was not what he wanted to say. What he wanted to say was: Why are we here? What about the words that captain had spoken in Sevastopol in 1974? What about all those great things we pretended existed? But he was a commander. His family lived in the naval housing blocks at Severomorsk. His political officer stood behind him. The communications he controlled, down on the seabed in the middle of the Pacific, were not encrypted.

To this day he did not know whether the Americans thought they needed no help, or thought the Soviets would never accept help, or whether the Americans simply knew: in these waters, the refusal left the Americans with the honor and the Soviets with the shame.

The submarine sank in the early hours of the next morning. Four crewmen died; the rest were rescued. Three weeks after his return to Murmansk, there was no medal and no commendation, only an internal security review from fleet headquarters, because a rear admiral suspected that “someone may bear responsibility for negligence in the accident.”

The Order of the Red Banner was put into the same box in the filing cabinet as the fleet newspaper from 1980, like some kind of metaphor. In the corridor, Katya said quietly, “Stop thinking about it. They want to get rid of you.” Sergei said, “They already have.” They had not. He had only realized that he could no longer pretend he had never seen the question.

———

August 19, 1991. Severomorsk.

A coup. The fleet commander ordered all vessels to readiness level two. Sergei was ashore; his submarine was still under repair, and he was at the base handling paperwork. He stood in the concrete square outside base headquarters and watched the sailors assembling on the docks. A young lieutenant ran past him wearing the expression Sergei himself had worn in 1974. Someone had told him something momentous was about to happen, and he believed it.

As he watched the young man run away, Sergei thought: That boy is running in the wrong direction.

That evening he went home and switched on the television. Tanks in Moscow were being broadcast live. Katya was cooking in the kitchen. For twenty-seven years she had cooked alone whenever he went to sea. She said nothing until Sergei said, “The fleet is still on standby.” She said, “You’re not going.” He said, “I have no boat. Nobody wants me.” Suddenly he realized that this was probably the first time in his life that saying “I have no boat” had brought him relief.

August 21. The coup failed.

When Sergei returned to the base the next day, someone had already taken down the banner that used to hang in front of Lenin’s statue—“The Northern Fleet, Heroes of the Soviet Union”—and piled it in a corner. Whether it had fallen and been trampled or someone had done it on purpose, he did not know. Nobody admitted anything. An old sailor bent down to pick it up, his face bewildered. He had held this cloth for twenty years, and then suddenly discovered it was only cloth.

———

December 25, 1991.

Now. Now he sat in the command compartment of K-219. The submarine had been struck from the register and lay in the scrapping basin. Next year it would be towed to Severodvinsk to be dismantled. The dents were still there: the explosion in 1987 had torn a two-meter wound in the outer hull. The boat could never be repaired, nor could the age that had made it.

He emptied the locker. In the shoebox were one Order of the Red Banner, one fleet newspaper from 1980, one unopened bottle of Armenian brandy, a diving knife sent from Kronstadt by a retired shipmate, and a photograph of Katya on her thirtieth birthday. Today he would take them home.

He did not cry. He was not a man who cried. But he did one thing: he spread out the 1980 fleet newspaper and, beneath the front-page headline “The Red Banner Northern Fleet—the World’s Most Powerful Nuclear Submarine Force,” wrote a small line in blue ballpoint with a pen someone had left behind:

“She was never that strong. None of us dared to say it.”

It was snowing outside. Darkness came early to Severomorsk in December; by half past three the sun was no longer visible. He tucked the shoebox under his arm, climbed the vertical ladder out of the command compartment, emerged from the hatch, and paused on the dock. Three submarines he had served alongside were moored there. Every one was coming apart, not physically, but because nobody maintained them anymore. Their hulls rusted, their antennas leaned, and nobody stood guard on the pier. Four months earlier there had been sentries with guns. The sentry was gone too. Three months behind on his pay, he had gone home to farm.

He stood on the pier looking south. South lay Murmansk, farther south Moscow, and farther south still Kyiv, his father’s birthplace. His father was Ukrainian, his mother Russian; he himself had grown up in Sevastopol. Ten years ago he was a Soviet citizen. Now he did not know what nationality he was, how he was to collect his retirement pay, or to whom the fleet he served in would be allotted next year.

The executive officer had called and asked whether he wanted to join the fishing company. “King crab in the Arctic Ocean are easier to find than American submarines, and canned crab sells for more than torpedoes in Sweden.” He had said, “I’ll think about it.” After hanging up, he stood there for three minutes before realizing it was not a joke. Seventeen years earlier they had thought they would die exchanging torpedoes with the Americans. Seventeen years later the executive officer was trawling for crabs, the commander was about to be unemployed, and the flag over the Kremlin had already come down.

He shifted the shoebox to his other arm and lit a cigarette. Katya was waiting at the base gate. He could see her headlights beyond the iron fence of the docks: a Lada they had bought in 1984, with a right headlight that was always going out. She wanted it fixed; he would not let her. “Let it be broken somewhere that doesn’t matter too much,” he said.

Katya rolled down the window. Much of her hair had turned white. She said nothing, only left the passenger door open.

He put the shoebox on the back seat. “Home.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.”

“You’re not leaving all those newspapers and things of yours on the boat?”

“No. Who still wants to read a navy newspaper that hasn’t written a word in thirty years?”

She said nothing. As the car left the base, the towers of the naval port turned gray-blue in the snow in the rearview mirror. December inside the Arctic Circle had no full day. After four, the floodlights on the pier lit up like two or three white stars. The radio was turned very low. A Moscow station was playing Tchaikovsky. The Soviet Union had dissolved, but the radio station remained: still Moscow Radio One, still Tchaikovsky. Some things are plainly gone, yet their lights stay on, and their last piece of music is still playing.

It was a long time before he told Katya what he had written on the fleet newspaper. It was the day his retirement hearing ended; the navy no longer needed sonar officers of his rank. Sitting in the passenger seat, she went through the shoebox he had brought home. She went straight to the photograph: not the Order of the Red Banner, not the group portrait of the diving unit, but the photograph of her thirtieth birthday, beside some park in Gdańsk. A pigeon had landed on her shoulder, and she was laughing, turning her face away because she thought it would peck her ear. It never did. The camera had simply clicked at that instant.

———

Later, Sergei did not go trawling for crabs. He found a job teaching sonar principles in the navigation department of a vocational college in Murmansk, earning the equivalent of 120 dollars a month at the 1993 exchange rate. On the classroom wall hung a faded nautical chart. It marked many of the places where American submarines rotated patrols, positions he himself had annotated ten years earlier.

A student asked him, “Mr. Belov, why didn’t we drive their submarines out?”

Sergei looked at the chart. He wanted to say many things. He wanted to say we could not catch them; even our propellers were twenty years behind theirs. He wanted to say that his proudest achievement in the Barents Sea was merely that, when he heard them, they had long since heard us. He wanted to say: the finest result of my life, its highest honor, was proving that I was no slower than somebody else.

But he said none of it. He only pushed up his glasses and looked at the student. “Underwater, you must keep silent. But when you speak to yourself, you must be honest. Can you do that?”

“Yes.”

“Then that is enough.”

———

He always kept that exquisitely sensitive ear. He could pick out the turbine of a Los Angeles-class submarine amid the countless noises of the sea. After 1983, nobody and nothing was worth listening to with such fine attention. His instruments slept longer than he did. Every nuclear reactor was shut down; every sonar array was taken down from the ceiling; every year the students asked the same questions. He kept listening, but heard nothing alive, until the year Katya became critically ill. Keeping watch by her monitor, he suddenly heard, inside its shrill cry, the Arctic Ocean of his youth beneath the surface: the low hum of an Ohio, the cracking of thick ice melting beyond the hull, Katya’s laughter on her thirtieth birthday.

He told no one. A sonar officer retired from a submarine struck from the register had heard the strongest reverberation of his life beside his wife’s hospital bed. He switched off the monitor’s low-noise alarm, took her fingers in his hand, and said: Katya, can you fucking please not go yet. She did not answer. From 1974 to the last moment of her life, it was the only call he ever made that went unanswered.

(The End)
