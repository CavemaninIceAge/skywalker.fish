Three in the morning. It is already September. The rain outside the window is fine, dense, and endless. I sit here thinking back over some of the major events of the past two months...

On June 21, I smashed an iPhone 12 in a fall in Changtu and slunk back to Harbin to wait for my results.

On the morning of June 25, I woke up full of excitement. That morning, a very close friend told me they had scored wonderfully high and said perhaps we could go to Peking University together. At noon, my mother came home with my results, which she had obtained early. My eyes landed straight on the final number: 655. Before I had fully taken it in, I excitedly checked again on Heilongjiang’s admissions portal. It was still the irrevocable 655. Later, my uncle called several times to ask. My mother’s replies were not particularly good, but I had no energy to correct them... For the next few days, I stayed alone in a hostel costing thirty yuan a night and probably answered no one’s messages.

June 28: with Guan, our city’s celebrated drunk, I “drank hard for half an hour and threw up all night.” In the early hours, I began vomiting blood. Only after four did I feel awake again. By then it was light.

On July 1, I learned I had not made the shortlist for Peking University’s Strong Foundation Program. Renmin University’s admissions office called and said I was likely to be admitted. I had been shortlisted for the University of Hong Kong’s principal nomination scheme. My mother sent me only a screenshot. I said nothing. Neither did she. And so HKU, for which we had paid a 300-yuan reservation fee, quietly slipped away. I walked the long way from Xuefu to Songpu Bridge. I hesitated for an hour, but in the end I did not dare jump. So I went to lie in the sun on Alejin Island. Every plant seemed extraordinary then: completely different leaves, flowers, and survival strategies, yet all revealing immense vitality in their competition. I do not know how much time passed before I was covered in sweat. That feeling was extraordinary too. If I laid my legs flat, I could be warm; if I raised my knees, I could touch the wind. Gradually I found the strength to sit up again, then walked from Harbin East back to No. 3 High School. Taking advantage of the campus open day, I secretly ate a carton of milk and a braised egg left beneath the Confucius statue to make up for the pain of being penniless and smelling delicious chive blossoms at the pet and flower market that I could not afford. (Oh, sorry, old Confucius.)

On July 2, I took the train back to Siping. Nine kilometers later, I crossed the Jilin–Liaoning provincial border and resumed my epic Harbin-to-Beijing bike ride. What followed was a rousing story of confronting the Liao River Plain and crossing the outlying ridges of the Yan Mountains. At the end, I charged through 800 li—400 kilometers—in two days, successfully cycling across Hebei Province and reaching Beijing on July 10!

On July 11, I flew back to Harbin in the morning and, just after landing, went to celebrate my friend’s birthday.

On July 18, a middle-school friend with whom I had weathered many storms returned to China after three years away. We all had a meal together. He invited me to go to Germany with him on August 10, which fit my plan to travel around Europe after finishing the Harbin-to-Beijing ride. When I got home, I asked my family how the visa arrangements were going. I had handed the whole task over to them in June. They said they had not done anything, so I had to start handling it myself from that point. (But I had reminded them twice in July, too.)

On August 6, I went to Shenyang to submit my visa application and was told I was missing an apostille. I could either go back to Harbin to obtain it or get the stamp at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs in Beijing. So I went to Beijing, where I happened to have lunch that afternoon with my big brother. Then came a stretch of ideal, peaceful, fulfilling days in Beijing.

On August 18, I returned to Harbin to prepare to repeat my final year of high school. Many people encouraged me, but the resistance at home grew stronger. After the second week, fierce arguments broke out over everything. I conceded that I would at least register at Beijing Normal University first, then withdraw before October if it did not work out.

On August 30, my mother secretly obtained a refund of the tuition at Deqiang High School, even though I had asked her not to cancel it yet.

September 1. University registration closes at six this evening. It is now almost four in the morning, and I am still far away in Harbin. Yesterday I once again lost the 1,400-yuan headphones I had brought back from Beijing. I also lost a set of my grandmother’s keys.

At this hour when the sky cannot decide whether to lighten, I have to admit that nineteen, which perhaps should have been the brightest year of my life, really is probably completely fucking ruined.

From HKU, to the visa, to repeating the year, I also have to admit that none of these failures were really caused by me, yet their consequences have embedded themselves deeply in my life...

“We must always choose one kind of failure to avoid a truly fundamental failure.”

When life suddenly turns from construction into firefighting, does planning the future become easier?

“When life gives you lemons, make lemonade.”
