At seven in the morning on July 18, Dad and I got up to head into the mountains. There were hardly any visitors at Yandang Mountain. Someone at the entrance was selling shuttle tickets between attractions. I scoffed at the idea, determined to walk between them myself. Once we entered the scenic area, though, I realized I had completely misunderstood how it worked. There is no general admission ticket at the entrance; instead, five main attractions are spread along both sides of the central route, with considerable distances between them. After stubbornly walking through the first attraction, we finally bought shuttle tickets.

Yandang Mountain is both a UNESCO Global Geopark and a national forest park, without the famous designation of World Natural Heritage or a Chinese National Geography accolade. That suggested to me that its significance was probably more geological than aesthetic, and that did seem to be the case. Wenzhou, Zhejiang, has put up many signs explaining how the rocks were formed. Geological forces are always immense and awe-inspiring. We walked sixteen kilometers of mountain paths that day but didn't climb Yungang Peak because of the weather. Dad probably felt the scenery was getting repetitive, so he suggested skipping more similar attractions that afternoon and heading to the foot of Huangshan instead.

Hangzhou is the railway hub of southeastern China. Yet getting from prosperous Wenzhou to Huangshan, there were surprisingly few connecting trains through other places. We hadn't researched things well enough and missed the 2:52 p.m. train. So we took a taxi to Taizhou, a train to Hangzhou, then another taxi from Hangzhou South to Hangzhou West, passing the beautiful West Lake, and just managed to make it to Huangshan North. Our accommodation looked very nice, but it was a long way from the route we would take up the mountain the following day.

We ate fermented mandarin fish and other dishes at a hotel called Dongpo Something. The food was really good.

The next day, we went up the mountain. The climb was long, but part of the joy of climbing is seeing the mountain from different angles. I have to say, Huangshan is extraordinarily beautiful. Strange rock formations everywhere, steep mountain trails. We climbed at least a thousand meters that day and walked sixteen kilometers. The higher reaches of Huangshan are gorgeous. Halfway up, we could already see a sea of mountains, but as we climbed higher, we found still better views. A reminder not to become complacent or stand still.

There is a particular pleasure in pushing your limits.

Today we wanted to go back up and see the remaining rock formations, but an unlicensed guide talked us into a scam attraction and a rafting trip. The raft kept spinning, and I felt sick.

I rode a Fuxing train. I like them. We passed Poyang Lake, which was truly magnificent. Today I realized:

One: plan ahead. Make a very detailed plan, paying attention to every step. Break it down to each individual action; know what you're doing.

Two: every judgment, good or bad, must stay faithful to the facts. For example, I kept thinking it might be possible to climb Yandang Mountain at night, even though every source said it was only open during the day. Because I didn't want things to turn out badly, I fantasized that perhaps something would change when we arrived, and built my plans around that imagined change.

Three: don't let people rush you into decisions. Pause for around ten seconds before acting. In terms of the overall expected outcome, this is definitely an advantage. I made two mistakes today. First, I didn't stay on the shuttle to Yandangshan railway station, which meant we missed the earlier train and had to spend about 280 yuan on taxis. If I hadn't also rushed to book a hotel at the foot of Huangshan, we could have saved another 170 yuan in fares.

Four: after completing a step, spend another ten seconds or so checking it. While editing an image for He Guangyi today, this check helped me notice that “0” should have been “0.0.”

Five: work efficiently, and make your time estimates reliable so people know what to expect. For example, when editing the image for He Guangyi today, I said I would finish before 8:15 p.m., then got straight to it and finished. That was right.

Six: don't overcomplicate problems. Break them down into practical details. Use a variety of broad perspectives to approximate reality. For example, I've recently learned to look at things through the lens of early humans: men's enjoyment of ball games can be seen as simulated hunting; wanting to go out when you're unhappy as a change of camp; male competition as a struggle to lead the tribe.

Seven: never spend money you don't need to spend. But don't base decisions on avoiding a twenty-percent markup; it will drastically reduce efficiency and won't be worth it. Spend where you should, save where you can!
