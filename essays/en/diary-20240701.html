All night, mechanically repeating commands to vast armies, playing a grand-strategy game until dawn at four. Perhaps I’m not addicted to games. Perhaps I simply resent going to bed on time like someone with a purpose. Perhaps I’m simply too unhappy with real life.

Last night I played football for three hours. An unusually warm pink sunset made the summer-evening pitch look impossibly gentle. For a moment, I felt that these people and this view before me were my entire world. On the way home I spent 27.8 yuan on McDonald’s, my first proper meal in three days and nights. That meal meant I was desperately hungry. Perhaps it also meant the beginning of facing life properly again.

The night before, I had also fallen heavily asleep at about this hour. That night, a description resonated deeply with me: “Both parents alive, yet living like an orphan.” They call it “emotional withdrawal born of disappointment.” That is who I am. I know I cannot change my parents. I know that when I’m hurting, they can offer neither help nor comfort. If I again choose to become hysterical like a baby, trying to beg for a little love and support, I am certain to end up in another round of arguments and irrationality. Especially in the hours after midnight, when everyone is asleep and the friends who are sometimes my only support have gone to bed too, there is nothing I can hold on to except myself. And sometimes it is not only late at night. It is the same in daylight. In those moments of complete misery and confusion, “sometimes my life is all desert, and when I grieve I cannot hold my own tears.”

Sometimes I think someone like me ought to take greater responsibility for himself, because nobody else can take responsibility for me. Yet when I picked up the big bottle of Coke in the kitchen at four this morning, it was the first time in a whole day back here that I had touched even a little of the feeling of home. What is home? I don’t know. But it certainly isn’t a collection of food and consumer goods, although at friends’ homes I have felt warmth in rooms full of just those things. I know my father never starts a conversation with me. Sometimes he ignores me even when I speak first. My mother is much the same. Too often, I can barely find familiarity or warmth in these rooms. Yet whenever I came back at night to the rented apartment beside No. 3 High School, even its white light felt like a warm yellow source of comfort and support. When I cycled to Minzhu Town to stay overnight, the town and rice fields outside the window were especially quiet. Even the plastic bars on the window frame seemed intensely concrete and real. I wondered vaguely what it would have felt like to grow up there. In that most peaceful of worlds, with not even the sound of a car outside, could a person stop hanging in midair? Could simply sitting, standing, or lying down be enough to settle you on solid ground? Later I realized it had nothing to do with where you were. It was only a feeling. We need something outside ourselves through which to recognize ourselves. It is just that the world I live in cannot yet bear the weight of my soul.

Then I thought: home must feel something like the football pitch beneath that warm pink sunset. It should be gentle, real, and sometimes enough, all by itself, to be everything.

I tell myself I should build relationships, find my interests, see the people I want to see, see the world I want to see, and work for a cause I love. Though stepping out means wandering into the void, only by stepping out can there be a future.

I like to write when I see no way forward. I need to write when I see no way forward. Whatever happens, I have to reach myself first before I can step outside. Though stepping outside means venturing, lost, into deep space, only by stepping out can there be a future.

Here we shall go, for a next time!

My passport still hasn’t arrived today. I asked my mother whether it had come. She said no. But delivery from Harbin’s exit-and-entry office to home takes at most a day, and I had asked her to watch for it. Surely she ought to have told me something at least two weeks ago. Once again I learned that unless I carry a thing through myself, my family will not help me get anything done. Sometimes I feel as though every day I face the old Jiajing Emperor, who did not attend court for thirty years and unfailingly let every memorial disappear without a trace. Our household administration resembles the nearly paralyzed bureaucracy of his reign. Though it never speaks of its own accord, at least there is still some money in the imperial treasury to keep me from ending up destitute or dead in the street.

But surely the world is not supposed to be like this. That is why I like to wander empty-handed through the morning markets in Daowai, ride my bicycle to sit on the ground in a county town, and go lie on the great island in the river in every season. I like seeing people’s hope of selling something, children’s curiosity about every new thing, and people bargaining over a few pennies. If I buy a little melon and sit on a shop’s small stool to eat it, an old man is sure to sit beside me and tell me, with great warmth, about his everyday life. On the island in the river, the plants’ endless variety of forms fills me with wonder too. Perhaps all their lives require is sunlight and pollination, yet they arrange carbon chains into structures we could not know in full in a lifetime, producing completely different leaves, completely different flowers, completely different strategies for survival. Yesterday at noon I sat on the railing of Songpu Bridge for a long time. In the end, I decided to lie down on Alejin Island instead. The grass stems and roots were so soft and dense that not a speck of dirt got onto my white T-shirt. And all I had to do was raise my knees to feel the cool wind over the great river.

I watched the bright summer-afternoon sunlight create a fantastical Tyndall effect in my room, with orange wooden furniture on three sides, just as it had for so many years before. Suddenly I realized: had I really lived here for eighteen years? There is summer and light every year, yet the mingled impression of summer and light always belongs to the most comfortable moments in my memory. Being very small and listening idly to the scrap collector’s clapper outside the window. Holding someone against me in a radiant summer. Leafing through my bright yellow Selected Works of Mao Zedong in a house filled with light. And now. I saw similar sunlight in the years in between, but it always served as a way to remember earlier times. Now this new light is marking a new point in my life.

What kind of life do I want? I tell myself I have to go out—out of this house, out of these stifling, unreal days. Though stepping outside means wandering into the void, only by stepping outside can there be a future. I tell myself to build relationships, find my interests, see the people I want to see, and see the world I want to see. I tell myself to keep longing for a free and prosperous life, and to persist in the pursuit of rights and fairness. The year 2028 is closer than 2018, but my heart is closer to 2018. In March of that year I wrote in my diary, “Whatever the future is like, we will choose hope.” Today I have finally written its second half: “Though stepping outside means wandering into the void, only by stepping outside can there be a future.”

I should pull myself together. I should keep searching for my life without a moment’s pause and find a place to settle that feels real and familiar. I should go to Siping today and resume my bicycle ride to Beijing. During the holidays, I should stay in the staff dormitory at my aunt’s workplace in Beijing. For those trapped in summer, only by running can you feel the wind.
