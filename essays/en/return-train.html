At last I lie down on the train. Cold air blows straight onto my head in the sleeper berth, making me worry about my illness. Once settled, I put on music. The first song is “Until the World Ends” from Slam Dunk. A good omen.

For the coming year, I will not see Beijing again. Beijing is vast. Beijing slips rapidly past the train window. I am like a newly mobilized soldier on a military train, plunging headlong into an irreversible, unknown future. There is still no one who both loves me and believes in me, and also has the breadth of vision to guide me into that future.

On the way to Beijing Railway Station, I listened to my grandfather recount his eventful years: from high-school graduate to completing a correspondence degree at Harbin Institute of Technology, then becoming director of an education commission. He praised his own diligence and seriousness at work. Just as I was about to get out, he said, “Our family ought to produce someone outstanding. And you have the talent.”

I like it here very much. During my last hour at home, my aunt and I, both ill, occupied two corners of the living room, with my grandparents in the other two. All of them are people whose minds are hard to change once made up, who speak when something needs saying and act when something needs doing. When you feel unwell, that overflowing energy can be rather noisy. But it is good, too. I hate Beijingers’ arrogance, yet I like that mentality of the strong: “definitely,” “I will,” “that is how it is.” There is no such atmosphere in my own home. Sometimes I think my parents do love me. They are simply too lazy: so often making do, muddling through, and constantly holding up my affairs. When my aunt spoke about the mother of her husband, a senior official in Beijing, I thought, my God, this sounds just like my parents—not very conscientious, always making do. And that quality drastically diminishes the value of all the money and care they pour into me.

These two weeks have made me much more relaxed and gentle. All the conflict and strife vanished. Being alive suddenly seemed a very happy thing. A friend from two years ago added me and said he guessed I had not got into Peking University. I did not argue much. In truth, I could also sense that I lacked a certain indefinable quality peculiar to students at Tsinghua and Peking University. Yet today I seem to have acquired it. I do not know whether this ease and gentleness, this so-called air of strength, can last when I return home. But someone who has seen the right answer can surely work his way back to it again and again.

Why give up Beijing Normal University? None of my relatives or friends has blamed me for it. They all feel it is already an excellent outcome. My major is good, my classmates are welcoming, and the university is well located. I am certain I could have a happy life there. But perhaps life should not be a compromise. Perhaps the longing to distinguish myself still haunts me. I know that at a more elite university I would be surrounded by truly inspiring classmates, and I am someone deeply affected by his environment. When I think back on the major periods of growth in my life, almost all the people who guided me eventually entered Tsinghua or Peking University. I want to learn from the best how to deal with people and the world. I want to live among people better than me. By now, the grief and resentment I felt when my results first came out have worn away. I simply keep believing, almost blankly, that at a better university I will find people better than me. With even a little desire to emulate those you admire, you can improve greatly and quickly—just as my habits and character have matured so rapidly during these past two weeks in Beijing. Four years is enough to shape the much longer life that follows.

I hate lines like “Anyone with a dream is extraordinary.” Too many lazy fools say them too, making the words themselves seem foolish.

Everything will go well.

With fate; with humanity.
