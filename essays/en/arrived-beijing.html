Today is one of the brightest moments of my life. Perhaps the brightest.

I may have experienced several great victories in my life, but most involved luck, or something like only partial mobilization: civilian industry at best, perhaps even the tiny fraction a medieval lord might muster in a preindustrial age. This struggle, though, was an immense undertaking to which I gave everything. For the first time in my life, I committed myself completely to a great project. Once it began, every other consideration had to serve the central goal. We could almost say it resembled the great powers’ total mobilization in the Second World War: every resource set in motion toward the ultimate objective. We absolutely had to win, shutting out all the supposedly rational, comprehensive objections from those talkative, seemingly literary intellectuals.

When we look back from some distance in the future, we will find that this ride was more than a simple victory. It was the beginning of new ways of striving. In time I will return to Harbin and bid farewell to this modern life, a life outside the mire, like other people’s. But someone who has seen the light cannot forget the light he has seen. I still want to call every part of my life from this point onward “a Beijing kind of life.”

Cycling to Beijing is a miniature rehearsal of my future. Repeating my final school year is a long reenactment of the ride. Compared with my former life in Harbin, they differ as Hearts of Iron differs from Europa Universalis. Only by experiencing it myself have I finally felt that difference concretely and truly. The latter simply reproduces the same long life: because the struggle lasts so long, we have to account for every aspect of it. In the former, countless sovereign states in various stages of inertia emerge from their different circumstances, preparing for an “inevitable war,” ready to pay a total price for a victory they must never abandon.

So I woke at seven and entered Tongzhou. The exhaustion of the mind moved into my limbs, changing from an abstract feeling into concrete pain. First my calves ached, then my backside, then my back, then my hands. It was as though an immense pain was waging a guerrilla campaign inside my body. Fortunately, my remaining strength was greater than the pain, so it could only roam about, fruitlessly announcing here and there that it still existed. The road continued with even greater monotony. The old alternation of village and countryside, countryside and village, disappeared beneath an endless mass of towns. It almost made you believe that all of North China was a suburb of Beijing. It was hot. There were more cars. I sweated. The buildings grew taller.

“What will the moment of victory actually be like?” I asked myself. In the previous day’s imagining, I would set out in the coldest part of the night, stand on Beijing’s soil at dawn, and turn to see a new sun rising over Hebei.

But I did not set out at dawn, and Langfang had no stretch of land broad enough to contain a sunrise. There were only endless buildings of different heights. After I entered Yanjiao, my aunt, who had come to meet me, had already parked two kilometers east of the Chaobai River. I insisted on seeing the end of the road, so we agreed I would go to the river to photograph the boundary sign. The “Beijing Boundary” sign was small, with a red background. I recorded myself crossing the finish line several times and chose the best take to announce the final victory. I took a roundabout route back and ended up an hour later than planned. My aunt had prepared flowers and a banner. Uncle Zhang Li took photographs beside us. At our feet stood the G102, kilometer 37 milestone. Behind us were some migrant workers. After helping the driver fetch water, they sat at a distance and watched. I seemed to read a distance in their faces, though they seemed never to show any expression at all.

My aunt and the driver had parked two kilometers east of the Chaobai River. Wanting to cross the actual finish line, I still went to the river and repeatedly recorded videos of arriving in Beijing. The “Beijing Boundary” sign was red but very small, unlike all the provincial and city boundary signs I had seen before. From the Chaobai River, China Zun was visible in the distance. By the time I returned, it was around eleven. Zhang Li saw me first, then my aunt. She handed me a bouquet and a banner with something like “Yu Tianxing has cycled for fifteen days to reach Beijing” written on it, and told Zhang Li to take good photos of us. Several migrant workers watched from a distance. Ironically, they seemed like me, looking on at the attention and service from others that I had never received. I took out a wrench to dismantle the bicycle. I was filthy from head to foot, covered in dirt and grease. When I crouched down, Zhang Li crouched too. My aunt told me to stand up, and Zhang Li continued taking the bicycle apart for me.
