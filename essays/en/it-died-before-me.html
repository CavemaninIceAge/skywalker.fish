—Ten years in the life of a Polish Solidarity activist

On December 25, 1991, the Soviet Union dissolved.

In a bar on Warsaw’s Old Town Square, Marek Kowalski raised a glass of vodka to the level of his eyebrows and told a group of strangers, “It died before me.” Then he drained it.

He was forty-seven. Ever since the militia broke his left leg in 1983, he had walked with a slight limp. He was not a soldier, a trade-union hero, or anyone important. He was simply a former electrician’s assistant at the Gdańsk Shipyard. Then he was fired. Then he spent six years working in an underground print shop. Then he was arrested three times.

Now he was a member of the Sejm of the Republic of Poland. His party was called the Democratic Union. In his desk drawer lay a yellowed union card bearing the word “Solidarność”—Solidarity. In its photograph he was thirty-two, still had his hair, and was thin as a nail, with the eyes of a cat cornered against a wall but unwilling to beg for mercy. Sometimes he took the card out for no particular reason, set it on the desk, and went on reading his papers.

———

December 13, 1981.

Marek woke at half past four in the morning to pounding on the door. He was living in a one-room apartment in Gdańsk. His wife, Anna, was seven months pregnant. Under his pillow lay a stack of untrimmed sheets for the underground edition of Solidarity Weekly. He did not try to hide the papers. He knew it was too late. He simply turned to look at Anna. She handed him his coat and said, “Food’s in the pocket.” Three words. She did not cry. Of everything he could think about that night, what hurt most was not being arrested. It was that Anna did not even dare to cry. In her own country, she did not dare cry when her husband was taken away, because she feared the neighbors might hear and he would suffer for it.

At the detention center, the militia captain questioning him was a Pole only a few years older than he was. Smoking the cheapest local cigarettes, the man opened Marek’s file and said, “Kowalski, you’re an electrician. You know electrical work, right? Where the wires go. How the current flows. A union isn’t a circuit. A union is a short circuit. You stick a screwdriver in, you think you’re fixing something, but you’re burning down the house.”

“The house is already burning, sir. I’m only asking for a fire extinguisher.”

The captain looked at him for three seconds, then closed the file. “Take him away.”

They beat him that night. It was not an interrogation. It was a beating. His left kneecap was shattered and two ribs were cracked. The men beating him were not the KGB. They were Polish militia. Poles beating Poles. This was the most important lesson he learned: Soviet rule was not a group of Russians ruling a group of Poles. It was a group of Poles ruling another group of Poles on the Russians’ behalf. What you hated was not Moscow. It was the structure that made you betray your own people.

———

Deep winter, 1988.

In an unheated print room on the outskirts of Gdańsk, Marek cranked a hand-operated mimeograph until his palms blistered. Outside it was twenty below zero. Inside, they could see their breath. They were printing issue 247 of Solidarity Weekly. The headline was “Wave of Strikes Sweeps the Nation.” The ink had frozen. They took turns holding the tins against their chests to thaw them with body heat.

Someone asked, “How does this end?”

Marek slapped a stack of freshly printed sheets onto the table. “How else? Either we die in this room, or it dies and we walk out. When we leave, the building stays. So does the mimeograph.”

Everyone knew what he meant by “it.” Not Jaruzelski. Jaruzelski was another man holding a screwdriver. Marek would remember for the rest of his life what Jaruzelski had said into the television camera when he declared martial law in 1981: “We do not want to fight our own people.” As though he had had a choice. Faced with Moscow and his own people, he chose Moscow. Then he called himself a tragic figure.

Marek never felt sorry for him. An electrician knew: you did not repair a short circuit. You cut it off.

———

April 5, 1989.

The Round Table Talks. Warsaw. Marek sat in the fifth row of the Solidarity delegation. He was not part of the core negotiating team. He was an “expert” assigned to a back row, responsible for passing Wałęsa notes on the privatization of the semiconductor industry. But he listened to everything. Across the table sat members of the United Workers’ Party’s Politburo, in suits and ties, speaking gently and using phrases like “the responsibility of history” and “the future of the nation.”

For the first time, Marek heard these people publicly say “pluralism” and “parliamentary democracy” while the Soviet Union still existed. He was not persuaded. He found it funny. Ten years earlier, these people had signed the martial-law decree. Now they were inviting them to sit down and discuss elections. They had not changed. They knew they were out of money. Soviet oil subsidies had stopped. Gorbachev had said, “You sort it out yourselves.” The United Workers’ Party suddenly discovered that without Moscow, its beliefs were worthless.

Marek wrote a sentence on a slip of paper: “They haven’t come to negotiate because they believe in democracy. They’ve come because they no longer believe in themselves.” He did not pass it forward. He folded it and put it in his breast pocket—the same pocket where Anna had put his food in 1981.

June 4, 1989. The election. Solidarity won. Not by a little. It won every seat open to competition. On television, the incumbent prime minister, Rakowski, looked as though someone had slapped him and he did not dare hit back. In the living room, Anna opened a bottle of East German beer smuggled in through her sister. Marek sat on the sofa, watching the vote totals scroll across the screen. He said nothing.

“You waited eight years. Why aren’t you smiling?”

“I’m waiting for that captain from 1981 to say, ‘The house is burning.’”

Anna did not ask any more. She understood. He was not celebrating a victory. He was waiting for the man who had beaten him to admit: you were right. The fucking house had been burning all along.

———

December 25, 1991.

Now. Now he stood in the bar on Warsaw’s Old Town Square, holding a glass of vodka. On the television, Gorbachev resigned. The Soviet anthem sounded at the Kremlin for the last time—not as a national anthem, but as a funeral dirge.

The screen cut to Moscow. The red flag came down. The Russian tricolor went up. A few young people in the bar whistled. Someone tapped a beer bottle with a spoon. Marek said nothing.

Beside him sat a middle-aged man who was also silent. The man wore an old jacket with cuffs rubbed pale. His fingers bore welding burns: clearly a worker. The worker said, “My old man fought the Soviets by the Vistula in 1944. Home Army. When the German People’s Commissariat for Internal Affairs arrested him as a German, the Soviets watched from the other bank and did nothing. He watched Warsaw being bombed flat. He never forgave the Russians. If only he’d lived to see today.”

Marek asked where he was.

“Last year. Cancer. He won’t see it.”

Marek raised his vodka, not to the people in the bar but to a Home Army veteran he had never met. He thought: your father waited his whole life to see the Soviet Union fall. When it fell, he had only just gone. One year short.

This was the cruelest lesson he learned: the thing you wait your whole life for may arrive only after you die. Not because you are unworthy. Not because you did not try hard enough. Fate owes you no reasonable timetable.

Then he thought of the detention room in 1981, and the captain who had asked, “Do you know what a short circuit is?” Suddenly he wanted very much to call that captain. Not to humiliate him. To ask: the thing you believed in is gone now. How are you spending tonight?

CNN’s live broadcast continued on the television. In Moscow’s Red Square, someone was hammering at the base of a Dzerzhinsky statue. The statue had already been toppled after the August coup. Rust had left a red tear streaking the plinth.

Of course, that was a shot they would never show. The world would never see a close-up of Gorbachev’s face as the statue fell. But Marek did not need to see it. He had outlived that man. He was not Andrei.

———

At one in the morning, Marek left the bar. Snow was falling in Warsaw, fine and light, settling on the cobblestones like flour. The Christmas lights in Old Town Square were still on. Before 1989, there had been no Christmas lights here. Christmas was illegal. Not illegal under the law, but in practice: to celebrate Christmas was to risk an entry in your file.

Anna came to pick him up. She was forty-eight. Half her hair had turned white. She still walked the way she had when he knew her in Gdańsk in 1981: unhurried, but with no intention of taking a single step backward. She asked whether he wanted to go home.

“I want to go to Gdańsk. See whether that print room is still there.”

“They tore that building down five years ago.”

“Then I want to see the ground it stood on.”

She was silent for a while in the car. Then she said, “You survived.”

“Yes.”

“It wasn’t your fault.”

“Yes.”

“That’s it.”

“That’s it.”

What he spoke was not really English. It was the stiff classroom English he had spent a year learning. But Anna knew he was repeating her words in English. She spoke Polish. He spoke English. Their son was asleep in his room.

The car crossed the Vistula. A thin layer of ice covered the river. He thought: this river froze in 1981 too. It froze just the same in 1991. The river did not care who was on the bridge. The people were there themselves. They knew for themselves that the water was flowing, that it was thawing.

———

Those young people whistling in the bar—Marek later remembered that they had been born in 1981. They were newborns when martial law was declared. They did not know what it felt like to have no passport. They did not know what it felt like to hold frozen ink against your chest to thaw it. In all their lives, no militiaman in a white shirt had stood at their door considering how to frighten them before knocking. When they raised their beer bottles to celebrate the Soviet Union’s dissolution, they were not celebrating some great political victory. They were thinking, “That thing is finally dead,” the way you might discover that the annoying old boiler in the attic had finally been removed.

Marek did not envy them.

He simply thought: they do not know how heavy something is because it was never placed on their shoulders. That is not their fault. That is our work. We took it off their shoulders. They ought to live lives that do not require printing newspapers in basements. They ought to feel that Christmas has always been legal.

He fell asleep in the car. Anna drove through Warsaw’s snowy night. An old Polish song played on the radio, a woman singing about how “after you left, the flowers bloomed.” When the Soviet flag came down in Moscow, the flowers bloomed.

But Marek did not see them for himself. He fell asleep that night, and that was all right. Sleeping—sleeping peacefully at an hour when you might once have been taken away, sleeping in a warm car seat beside someone you love—that feeling itself was the sound of the Soviet flag coming down that day.

(The end)
