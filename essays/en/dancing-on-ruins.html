—A young Soviet rocker’s 1991

In August 1990, Viktor Tsoi died.

When Misha heard the news in a Leningrad basement, he was prying open a dubbed cassette of Blood Type with a screwdriver. He had played it so many times that the felt pad had worn out and the tape had jammed. He put the screwdriver on the table, went out, and stood in the stairwell for a long time. He did not cry. It was something more physical, as though someone had pulled something out of his chest.

Tsoi was his only evidence. Proof that a young Soviet person could live without lying.

Misha was twenty-two. On paper, he studied mechanical engineering at the Leningrad Institute of Technology. In reality, his entire education took place in three places: a Sony Walkman his cousin had brought back from Finland, the semi-underground shows on Vasilyevsky Island, and every unauthorized Aquarium performance “rocking” the Leningrad Youth Palace. His father was a Communist Party member and an engineer. Whenever he said at dinner, “This Western musical garbage is corrupting you,” Misha thought: that tractor engine you designed hasn’t gone into production in seventeen years. Who exactly is corrupting whom?

He was not an anti-Soviet dissident. He simply felt that this world was unreal. Television announced that production targets had been exceeded. The shops had no meat. Newspapers said imperialism was collapsing. Every cassette he wanted to hear had been smuggled in from an imperialist country. At school, they said Soviet youth were the most idealistic young people in the world. Every idealistic young Soviet person Misha knew was trying to save enough foreign currency to go to Warsaw and buy cut-out tapes.

In 1989, he formed a band with two friends. Sasha, the guitarist, was a welder at the Baltic Shipyard. Igor, the drummer, sorted parcels on the night shift at the post office. That post office was probably the most poorly supplied institution in the Soviet Union, yet every smuggled tape in the city had been “handled” by Igor somewhere among its parcels. They had no bassist, because they could not find a working bass guitar anywhere in Leningrad. They rehearsed in Sasha’s sixth-floor apartment, stuffing quilts into the windows—not because they feared the KGB, since it was 1989, but because the neighbors would report the noise to the police.

They called their band Empty Shelves. Their first original song was called “Queue.” Its lyrics were just four lines: “Queue for butter. There is no butter. Queue for hope. There is no hope. Keep queuing, because what else can you do?”

Misha never thought he was doing politics. He was only putting what he saw outside his window into songs. Then he discovered that in the Soviet Union, simply refusing to lie meant you were already doing politics.

———

Spring 1991. Radio stations began playing songs that had previously circulated only underground. DDT, banned just the year before, actually appeared on state television. One day Igor came running over, excitedly waving a newspaper. “Look! Komsomolskaya Pravda interviewed Boris Grebenshchikov!” Komsomolskaya Pravda. Interviewing the lead singer of Aquarium. Reality was more psychedelic than any drug.

But Misha was wary. He had never trusted anything handed down from above, even when what they handed down was something he used to hide beneath his bed. He had an instinct: just because a system lets you listen to rock does not mean it has changed. Perhaps it needs your help to look as though it has. During rehearsal he told Sasha, “I don’t trust this glasnost. I think they’re putting us on display.”

“Being an exhibit is better than being an enemy. Do you know how many years they could have given us for rehearsing here this time last year?”

Misha had no answer.

He did not trust the regime. He did not trust reform either. He trusted only Tsoi, and Tsoi was dead. A few months before his death, Tsoi had written a song called “Changes,” with the line “Our hearts are waiting for change.” Young people across the Soviet Union carved the words into school desks, stairwell walls, even the doors of military trucks. But Misha felt no one had really understood the song. It was not an optimistic song. When Tsoi sang of “change,” he was not expressing anticipation. He was saying: we have waited so long. Will we ever live to see it?

———

August 19, 1991.

When news of the coup reached Misha, he was in the sorting room at Igor’s post office, going through a parcel sent from Helsinki. Inside was a newly pressed death-metal demo. Igor waved and said he could have it. Then he said, “Have you heard the radio? There’s been a coup.”

Misha’s first reaction: fuck. His second: are we still rehearsing that evening? His third: I’m going out into the streets.

On the evening of August 20, he went with Sasha and Igor to Palace Square. It was full of people. They had not been organized by the government. They were not marching. They were simply standing there. Thousands of people, some holding candles, some holding handwritten scraps of paper. Someone was singing an Aquarium song. Someone was shouting, “No tanks.” A woman in her forties—the sort of Soviet auntie who had spent half her life queuing—held a piece of cardboard with words written in fountain pen: “Communism in our lifetime. That was their promise. What did they give us?”

Misha looked at the woman. Suddenly he thought: my mother has never written anything on a sheet of paper. She has never in her life written anything on a sheet of paper.

Then he heard a guitar.

Someone had brought a six-string. A small group gathered, and someone began playing a Kino song. Not “Changes,” but “Blood Type”: “My blood type is on my sleeve, my number stamped on my arm.” Misha sang along. As he sang, he discovered he was crying. At the time he could not understand why. Was it happiness? The woman’s cardboard sign? Because Tsoi had died without seeing this day?

He felt it was none of those things.

Only later did he understand: this song used to be sung underground, in Sasha’s apartment with quilts blocking the windows. Now it was being sung in a square, surrounded by strangers, with no one afraid and no one hiding. He had cried because the conditions for singing it had changed, not because of the words. The song itself was the same. Once it had needed to be hidden. Now it did not. In that moment, Misha knew that a world which required you to hide your music might really be coming to an end.

———

August 21, 1991.

The coup failed. The three of them watched the news on a fourteen-inch black-and-white television in Igor’s dormitory. The coup committee had dissolved. Tanks were withdrawing from Moscow’s streets. Yeltsin had signed an order suspending the activities of the Russian Communist Party. The leaders of the Soviet Communist Party were being arrested.

Sasha took the remote and changed the channel. It was not another Soviet station, but a Western MTV tape playing on a VCR stolen back from Helsinki. On the screen was Nirvana’s “Smells Like Teen Spirit.”

“Lenin would die of rage all over again.”

“Definitely. A brain hemorrhage. At the sight of Kurt Cobain’s long hair.”

Misha made no joke. He was thinking about a question: now what?

The coup had failed. The Communist Party had been suspended. Everything he had been told all his life he had to believe, and had never believed, was being dismantled alive on television. He ought to be happy. He was happy. But mixed into that happiness was something he could not quite describe: “And then what?”

The thing he had rebelled against his whole life was gone. All his songs—the songs he had written hidden in basements, about empty shelves and queuing—had lost their subject. A boxer had spent ten years hitting a punching bag, and suddenly the bag had flown away.

He had always written songs for things that were forbidden. Now nothing was forbidden. Were the songs still real?

“Let’s record a proper EP next week. Nobody’s stopping us now. We can get into a studio.”

“We can get into a studio. There’s nothing left to hide. What do we write?”

“Write what Tsoi would write if he’d lived to see today.”

———

December 25, 1991.

The Soviet Union was gone.

Misha sat in front of the same fourteen-inch black-and-white television, watched five minutes of Gorbachev’s resignation speech, then turned to a cassette of Motörhead’s 1916 he had just acquired. Motörhead had released the album that year. There were only a dozen or so copies in Leningrad, and he had one. He muted the television—whatever that middle-aged man was saying did not matter anyway—and pushed the tape into his Sony Walkman.

In his headphones, Lemmy sang about being sixteen in 1916 and dying on the Somme.

Then he thought: my grandfather died on a battlefield too. He fell in Berlin in 1945. He was nineteen, a Hero of the Soviet Union. His medal hangs in my father’s living room. Now the country he died for in Berlin has signed a piece of paper and disappeared. Not because it lost a war, but because it no longer wanted to exist.

Misha switched off Motörhead, picked up a pen, and found his Moleskine notebook. Imports of such things had still been banned in the Soviet Union the year before. A Finnish girl had slipped it to him when she came to the Leningrad rock festival. He wrote a line in it:

“They spent seventy-four years building a house with no foundations. Then everyone moved in and pretended it would never fall. It fell. We dance on the ruins—not in celebration, but because what else can you do but dance?”

Misha’s song would eventually be called “The Finnish Girl’s Notebook.” Thirty years later, his daughter would find a restored version on YouTube, open the comments, and discover that someone in a Ukrainian bomb shelter was listening too.

But that was later.

———

After the dissolution, Misha did not become anyone remarkable. He was not Yeltsin, not Berezovsky, not an oligarch harvesting wealth from the ruins of the old Soviet Union. He simply kept doing what he had always done: writing songs in rehearsal rooms and touring Europe. No one stopped him anymore.

It took him a long time to understand that his generation’s good fortune was not that they had “won.” It was that the thing they needed to resist had fallen by itself before it could bring them down. He had not spent half his life, as his father had, drawing blueprints for a country that no longer existed. In a sense, his father’s life had been part of that building with no foundations. Misha no longer had to live inside it. This was the greatest and cruelest piece of good fortune separating him from the Andreis of the world.

He had not become braver. He had simply lived long enough, and he had always had a guitar in his hands.

Those songs once had to be hidden. Later, they did not. Later still, Misha grew old. When he went to his son’s school for a parents’ meeting, he heard two children in the corridor singing “Blood Type” on a karaoke app. They did not know what the song had once meant. They just liked the melody.

Misha stood in the corridor for three seconds.

He did not cry. It was neither sentiment nor sorrow.

It was a feeling he could not describe even to himself: the words you once needed courage to say had become a melody they could sing without thinking. You thought you had lost. You were only waiting.
