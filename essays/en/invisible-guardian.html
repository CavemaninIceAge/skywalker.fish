“Will our children still believe in a young man named Xiao Tu?”

It reminds me of The Message, which I watched two nights before my high-school entrance exam:

“I am not afraid of dying. I am afraid that the people I love will not know why I died.”

“Here in purgatory, I leave this record in the hope that my family and Sister Yu will forgive the decision I make now. But I firmly believe you will eventually understand my heart. My loved ones, I am so heartless toward you only because our nation stands on the brink of survival or destruction. We can only risk ourselves without hesitation for the slightest chance of saving it. My body will soon perish, but my soul will remain with you.”

And The Invisible Guardian says:

“Revolution always comes with bloodshed and sacrifice. To die for one’s beliefs is a kind of happiness.”

Because peace and independence have already cost bloodshed and sacrifice, justice and freedom deserve all the more to be cherished and protected. Protecting them is our historical responsibility as humanities students.

We humanities students must make the world a better place: not only wealthier and faster, but more humane, more civilized, more governed by law, and more loving toward all.

Sometimes I think that if I lived in a country with a different political tradition, perhaps I would want to become a freelance editorial writer, an investigative journalist, or the head of a nongovernmental nonprofit organization. Instead of what I am doing now: yearning for chances to sell out my character in exchange for a marriage to the will to power...
