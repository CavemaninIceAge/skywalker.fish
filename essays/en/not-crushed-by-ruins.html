At ten this morning, Dad and I set off on our tour of the great mountains. We arrived in Changchun at 11:30; our flight from Changchun Longjia Airport was not until 15:30. At Harbin West Station I suggested, “A new six-story shopping mall has opened at Jilin University’s Qianwei South Campus. Shall we eat there?” Dad was unusually interested in visiting his old university, and that made me happy too.

When we came out of the metro station on Jiefang Avenue, Dad did not know the way to the university and even misread the map. Only then did I realize he had not returned for twenty years to what he called “a place of sorrow.” He was clearly more interested in the former Changchun College of Geology, where he had worked, than in Jilin University’s Qianwei North Campus, where he had studied. I asked what kind of institution the geology college was. He said it was at the lower end of the top admissions tier, and that he had taught university physics there, specifically mechanics and geometrical optics. He recognized none of the tall glass buildings on either side of the street. At the Geological Palace in the old Cultural Park he went to look through the entrance, unable to ask the guard openly and confidently to let him onto the campus where he had once taught. I asked whether we could go in. The guard said no. Dad said nothing and leaned against the glass to look inside. The guard said, “Don’t put your hands on the glass. Someone will have to clean it.” Dad said, “Yes, yes, I understand.” But where he had just lowered his hands, the oils from his skin had left clear prints. I took out a tissue to wipe them away, but they would not come off. Dad held the window frame on the other side and, in a voice slightly louder than the place called for, explained the lavish Soviet-designed heating system inside. As we left, I asked whether the guard could make an exception. He waved a hand without a word.

After Yu Yangpeng left Changchun twenty or twenty-five years ago, he never returned, never saw any of its buildings or teachers again. Given his surprising description of it as “a place of sorrow,” his return to Harbin may have been a turning point brought about purely by calamity, no kind of considered trade-off. Bethune Medical University stood opposite Jilin University. The story Grandma told about a female student there was probably true. I imagine that in some other chain of causes, Dad should have been a physics professor at Jilin University, and I would have had a clever, slender doctor mother with a delicate face, a graduate of Bethune Medical University and a member of the professional elite. Or in a grander dream, I would have had a father who owned a huge internet company, a father who made a fortune trading after the collapse of the Soviet Union. In the years when China first connected to the internet and the Soviet Union broke apart, those opportunities had been so close to him. But with obstacles such as his family refusing to give him money, he stayed one step from countless chances to cross class boundaries, never daring to take it. Mum says Dad is a kind man. Perhaps that, too, made him unable to insist on his choices when fate pressed him, and so he never made that leap in social class during those golden forty years. Meanwhile, his former roommate M became a physics professor at Peking University. Others went into water engineering or became university professors in Panzhihua, and another became the owner of a technology company.

However one looks at it, this is a sad story, sad enough to overturn an entirely different possibility for a life. Rather than treat it as a tragedy whose effects continue today, I would rather see it simply as a lesson. I think that sooner or later, everyone encounters a major blow. What matters is how you respond, how you stand up after the impact, and whether you become someone who can no longer be knocked down and knows exactly who they want to be. Once someone loses to that blow, they seem almost destined for a lifetime of spiritual impotence. Life ceases to be an act of creation and becomes a series of ever deeper compromises. The eras that follow have no names of their own. They are all called “the time after that thing happened.”

For L, that thing may have been his University of Toronto offer arriving before his visa, or perhaps his earlier failure in the high-school entrance exam. For S, it may have been missing out on No. 3 High School and going to Deqiang instead. For W, it may have been his dyslexia. In struggles where even their utmost efforts could only narrowly beat fate, they made different choices and chose different presents for the futures that followed.

Fortunately, after 2020 I gradually rebuilt myself from the ruins. I became someone who had a future, and created a new, vigorous spirit.

At half past two we returned to Changchun Station to take the train to the airport. In the end Dad never entered the Qianwei North Campus where he had studied. On Liutiao Street he only photographed his old dormitory and sent the picture to M. Professor M used to climb in through the window from the shed roof. But I suspect Dad could neither take a good photograph nor compose an amusing line to go with it, and Professor M probably did not know what to say to him either.

We flew from Longjia to Beijing Daxing, and the later flight was delayed. Even so, Dad and I were happy. At 00:30 we finally reached Wenzhou. Our ride-hailing driver took us from the airport to the Yandang Mountain visitor center without demanding a return-trip surcharge. That, to me, is civilization.

Beyond the window, which had no insect screen, stood distant mountains. From the slightly damp room we could glimpse one imposing corner of Yandang Mountain. The day was over.
