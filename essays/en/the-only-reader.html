—Twenty-Six Years in the Life of a Leningrad Writer

December 25, 1991. St. Petersburg.

Ilya Samoilov, sixty-one, climbed the steps of 4 Liteyny Avenue. People in Leningrad called it the Big House. There was a joke: it was the tallest building in the city—you could see Siberia from its basement. The last time he had entered that door was in 1965. That time, someone had brought him in.

A woman in her forties from the archives sat in the reception room. On her desk lay a kraft-paper dossier tied with string. The wax seal on the string had already broken. On the cover were a case number and a line of text: One Hundred and Twenty-Five Grams, manuscript, first copy, 412 pages.

“Sign here, and it's yours.”

“It was mine to begin with.”

“Then sign a little faster. We don't even know who we'll answer to this afternoon.”

He signed. She pushed the dossier toward him. He did not open it there. He tucked it under his arm, went down the steps, walked to the Neva, stood by the river for a while, and then went home.

That evening, the whole country watched a man reading from a script. He didn't watch. He was reading a manuscript someone had read twenty-six years earlier.

———

December 25, 1941. Leningrad.

Ilya was eleven. That day, the bread ration increased: for dependents, from 125 grams to 200. His mother returned from the bakery with a paper parcel larger than usual. She began crying on the stairs.

His sister had died on November 19. Her ration card expired at the end of the month, and his mother used it until the end of the month. The card was a gray sheet printed with squares; one was cut off each day. After his sister died, the squares were still cut off. Eleven extra days, 125 grams each day. His mother cut the bread into three portions: one for him, one for herself, and one placed on the bed his sister had slept in, to be divided again that evening. She said it was his sister's. What they were eating was his sister's.

The book he later wrote, all 412 pages, was about those eleven days. There were no heroes in it, no artillery fire, no “Leningrad held firm.” Only a room, a bed, three portions of bread, and a card that expired at the end of the month.

The book was called One Hundred and Twenty-Five Grams.

———

April 1965. Leningrad.

He was thirty-five, a member of the Writers' Union, with a published novella about a construction site that had been well received. One Hundred and Twenty-Five Grams had taken six years to write. He typed three copies: the first for Zvezda magazine, the second for himself, the third made with carbon paper. That one was faint; in places you had to hold it to the light to read it.

The editor at Zvezda read it and said it was the best thing written about the siege in twenty years. Then he said, “I'll have to send it up for approval.”

It spent three weeks higher up. At two in the morning on April 14, someone knocked. Four men, two in uniform, two without. They searched for five hours, took the first and second copies, took the drafts, took the carbon paper, took the typewriter. It was a portable Moskva. They said they needed to check the typefaces.

His mother was there. She was sixty-one; she had been thirty-seven during the siege. She stood in the kitchen doorway, watching them fill their boxes, saying nothing. As they carried the typewriter out, she said one thing: “It's my daughter you're taking.” One of the uniformed men paused. He did not turn around.

The third copy wasn't in the apartment. It was downstairs at Nina Vasilyevna's, inside her piano stool. Nina Vasilyevna had been their neighbor during the siege. She had helped his mother carry his sister downstairs. He had left the third copy with her not because he had foreseen anything, but because she had said she wanted to read it. She read for two days. When she finished, she put it in the piano stool and said, “Leave it with me for now.”

At seven in the morning, they took him to the Big House. A major conducted the interview, very politely, speaking like an editor. The major said the book was well written. The major said it was precisely because it was well written that it could not be published. The major said the siege was heroic, and that had already been decided, neither by you nor by me. The major said this book would not be published. Not “not now.” Not ever.

The major asked who had read it. He said the editor. The major asked who else. He said no one. The major asked whether he was certain. He said he was.

It was the most important lie he ever told. The major wrote it down.

Then the major said, “You may go.”

He went. He never understood why he had been allowed to.

———

1966–1988. Leningrad.

The Writers' Union expelled him for “problems with his creative stance.” The meeting lasted forty minutes. Eleven people raised their hands; two did not. The two who didn't never spoke to him afterward either. He found work as a night watchman at a publisher's paper warehouse. It was filled with rolls of paper for printing books, each two meters high. Every night he made three rounds among them, then sat by the door. The paper belonged to the state. Every roll had a purpose. Not one was his.

He did not write a word. For twenty-two years.

It wasn't fear. He had come to understand something: if you wrote something a second time, it became a different book. He did not want to write a different book. That book was finished, in the Big House, 412 pages. He had no intention of writing it again.

Nor did he go to Nina Vasilyevna's to collect the third copy. To collect it would be to acknowledge that it existed. In 1979, Nina Vasilyevna died; her daughter inherited the piano. He attended the funeral and said nothing about the stool. He didn't know whether its contents were still there. He didn't ask.

He drank, but not much. He lived. A writer who does not write lives much as a writer who does. There is just one thing missing.

His mother died in 1974. Before she died, she asked once: “Where's the book?” He said, “In the Big House.” She said, “It's cold there.”

In the late seventies, a young man came to the paper warehouse looking for him. He was in his early twenties, a poet, part of the crowd at the Saigon café. The young man said, “We've heard about your book.” He said, “There is no book.” The young man said, “Everyone says there is.” He said, “No one has read it.” The young man said, “That's why it exists.”

A book nobody had read had been famous in this city for twenty years. He did not know what to do with that fact.

———

November 1988. Leningrad.

Zvezda called. A new editor-in-chief, young, his voice urgent. “Samoilov,” he said, “we're going through the rejected-submission files from the sixties. There's an entry: One Hundred and Twenty-Five Grams, submitted for review, not returned.” He asked, “Do you still have a manuscript?”

“Do you know we can publish anything now?”

“I know.”

“Then why didn't you come to us?”

“I don't have a manuscript.”

He went to Nina Vasilyevna's daughter's apartment. The piano was still there, and so was the stool. Her daughter said, “No one's opened that stool in twenty years. It's full of my mother's sheet music.” Beneath the sheet music lay 412 carbon-copy pages. They were wrapped in a May 1965 issue of Leningradskaya Pravda. The front page marked the twentieth anniversary of victory; that year, May 9 had become a public holiday for the first time. A book about the siege, wrapped in a newspaper celebrating victory, had lain in a piano stool for twenty-three years. The letters were faint. In forty-seven places, even holding them to the light was no longer enough.

Zvezda published it in its third issue of 1989, with eleven sentences removed. The editor said those eleven went too far; readers would not be able to bear them. The forty-seven illegible passages were printed as three dots inside square brackets. The print run was 300,000.

More than four hundred letters came. One was from a seventy-year-old woman: “My mother used my little brother's card too. For six days. I've never told anyone.”

Another was from a man: “You're lying. The siege was heroic. My father died there.” He put this letter beside the other one. He felt both were true. A million people had died in the siege, each in a different way. Some were heroes. Some were simply hungry.

He read that letter many times. Perhaps this was what publication meant, he thought. He did not feel free. He felt that a book missing eleven sentences and forty-seven words was in the hands of three hundred thousand people.

———

December 25, 1991.

Now. Now the dossier was on the table, its string untied.

The first copy. The one he had typed himself, the letters black and clear. All 412 pages, not one missing. First he looked for the forty-seven passages. They were all there. In pencil, he copied them one by one into the magazine. It took two hours. When he finished, he closed the magazine. The book was whole.

There was also an inventory in the dossier, dated April 14, 1965, listing the items taken. The final entry: one Moskva typewriter. Beside it, in small letters: destroyed. They had destroyed the typewriter and kept the manuscript. What they kept was evidence. What they destroyed was the tool.

Then he went through it from the beginning.

Someone had read it with a red pencil. Not crossing things out, but drawing lines beneath the sentences. In the top right corner of the first page was a date: April 15. In the bottom right corner of the last page, another: April 18. Someone had read it in four evenings.

He counted the underlined passages. Eleven. The same eleven sentences the editor had removed in 1989. The red pencil and the editor's blue pencil had marked the same sentences. One said they were evidence; the other said they went too far. Their eyes were equally precise.

On the last page, below the date, was another line in pencil. The letters were tiny. Whoever wrote them had not intended anyone to see:

“Read. Recommend withholding publication. No action against the author.”

He sat there for a long time. When the major in 1965 had said “You may go,” he had thought it was luck. For twenty-six years, he had believed it was luck. It was not luck. Someone had read his book over four evenings and then written a line on the last page. The most attentive reader of his life had decided to let him live.

He did not know who that person was. He did not intend to find out.

———

January 2, 1992. St. Petersburg.

That day, prices were freed. Bread prices rose by three times. He went to buy bread and queued for forty minutes. When he returned, the paper parcel in his hand was smaller than usual. He could not afford a typewriter. He wrote by hand.

He put the first manuscript in his desk drawer—the same drawer it had been in before they took it in 1965. He closed the drawer. From now on, a drawer was only a drawer.

Then he took a sheet of paper and sat down. He was sixty-two and had not written a word in twenty-six years. He would not write the same thing twice. This was not a second time. This was a second book.

He wrote the first sentence. His hand was steady.
